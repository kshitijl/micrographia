The evidence for atoms: fluctuations and light

Why is the sky blue? And why does a fluid turn milky at its critical point?

A perfectly even medium would scatter no light at all: every bit of it would be cancelled by another. Light is scattered by unevenness, and a gas made of molecules is never even: by chance, some patches hold a few more molecules than others. How much, and on what scale, decides the colour of the sky and the milkiness of a fluid near its critical point. Here is a fluid of molecules on a grid, and the light it would scatter.

Press Run. Very hot (kT ≳ 3), the molecules are spread nearly at random: the unevenness is small and the same at every scale, and the scattered light is blue. Cooler, neighbours start to clump a little. Cool it towards the critical point: patches of crowding and emptiness grow, as big as the light's wavelength, and the scattered light gets bright and white.

Why blue

A molecule shaken by light re-radiates it, more strongly the faster it is shaken: the scattered intensity goes as 1/λ⁴, so blue light (λ ≈ 450 nm) is scattered about six times more than red (700 nm). But that is only half the story. In a perfectly even medium the waves scattered from different places cancel, and none comes out sideways: that is why clear glass and pure water look clear. What survives is the scattering from unevenness: the amount is proportional to the mean square excess of molecules in a patch the size of the wavelength, S(k). In a gas the molecules are scattered at random, so a patch holding N molecules on average holds N ± √N, and S is 1 at every scale. So the sky is blue: the 1/λ⁴ of each molecule, times the random-number unevenness of air.

Counting molecules from the sky

The √N matters: the fewer and bigger the molecules, the more uneven the air for the same density, and the brighter the sky. Rayleigh (1899) and Einstein and Smoluchowski (1910) turned this into a formula, and comparing the sky's brightness with the sun's gives the number of molecules per cubic centimetre of air: Perrin's table lists about 6 × 10²³ per mole from it, in agreement with his Brownian grains.

Why milky at the critical point

Near the critical point a fluid can hardly decide between liquid and vapour, and patches of each come and go on every scale up to a "correlation length" that grows without limit as T approaches Tc. The unevenness S(k) at long wavelengths becomes huge, at visible wavelengths too: the fluid scatters light thousands of times more than a gas, and, because the patches are as big as the light's wavelength, it scatters all colours about equally. So it turns milky white: critical opalescence, which Andrews saw in carbon dioxide in 1869. The measured brightness readout compares the scattering with an ideal gas's at the same density.

Caveats

  • A 2D lattice gas (the Ising model), not air or carbon dioxide, and its "wavelengths" are scaled to the lattice: blue is a wave 43 sites long, red 65, keeping visible light's ratio (1.5), so that the patches near the critical point can match them. Real light's wavelengths are hundreds of molecules long.
  • The colour shown is the scattered light for white light coming in: intensity in each colour ∝ k⁴ S(k).
  • Monte Carlo moves (Kawasaki): a heat bath at the set temperature; the molecules' number is fixed at half the sites, the critical density. Molecules only hop to neighbouring sites, so the long-wavelength patches take longest to form, and near the critical point much longer still (critical slowing down): give it time after each change, at full speed.
