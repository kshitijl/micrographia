Evaporative cooling, and the wet-bulb limit

Why does sweating cool you down, and why does it stop working when the air is humid?

A film of water on hot skin, in hot air, ends up colder than the air. Nothing cold touched it. The molecules that escape from the water are the fastest ones, and they take their energy with them, leaving the slower ones behind. But that only works if they can escape: in air already full of water vapour, as many come back as leave, and the cooling stops. That is why a heatwave at 35 °C and 80% humidity can kill, and the same heat in a desert need not.

Run, with dry air. The water cools below the air above it, and stays there: the heat flowing in from the warm air just pays for the evaporation (the wet-bulb temperature). Turn the fan on: the dry air comes closer, and it cools more. Now raise the humidity: the cooling fades, and at 100% it is gone.

The fastest leave first

To leave the liquid a molecule must climb out of its neighbours' pull, which takes much more energy than an average molecule has (the latent heat: about 4 ε a molecule here, ten times kT). Only the fast tail of the speed distribution makes it; every one that escapes takes more than its share of the energy, and the rest re-share what is left: a lower temperature. (The same tail is why warm air holds more water.) Here nothing heats or cools the water except the air above it and its own evaporation, so its temperature is the balance between the two: the wet-bulb temperature, which meteorologists measure with a thermometer wrapped in a wet cloth.

Why humidity matters, and wind

Molecules also come back: vapour in the air lands on the water and is caught. In saturated air (100% humidity) as many land as leave, the net evaporation is zero, and the water stays at the air's temperature. Your body makes heat all the time and sheds much of it this way; when the wet-bulb temperature reaches about 35 °C, it cannot shed it at all, and a person in the shade, wet and fanned, still overheats. A fan helps for the other reason: it sweeps away the damp layer of air next to the skin, so the air the water sees is drier.

Caveats

  • 2D Lennard-Jones "water" (it evaporates far more readily than real water, so the cooling is bigger and faster than real sweat's), under purely repulsive "air".
  • The wind is a rule of the page: in the band near the ceiling (deeper with the fan on), water molecules are turned into air molecules, or air into water, every 5 τ, to hold the vapour there at the set humidity. Energy is kept (the molecules keep their speeds; in the thin air their pull on each other is negligible).
  • No body heat: the water has no heater under it.
