Einstein, 1905: Brownian motion

Why do pollen grains jiggle in water? And how did that jiggling count atoms?

A grain five molecules across, in a liquid, in 3D. Nothing pushes it but the molecules around it, a few hundred hitting it at once from every side, never quite balanced. Follow it under the microscope; then throw it and feel the liquid hold it back. Einstein's 1905 insight was that the jiggle and the drag are the same collisions, so together they reveal the temperature, and from it how many molecules make up a gram.

The jiggle

The grain is struck all the time from every side, and the blows almost cancel; what is left is a small, random, ever-changing push, and the grain wanders. Look more often and the path is no smoother: each straight segment, seen with a finer Δt, becomes a zigzag as tangled as the whole ("a polygonal contour relatively just as complicated as the whole figure", Perrin wrote). That is why early observers who timed the grains' "speed" got nonsense: there is no speed to measure, only a spreading. The grain's distance from where it started grows as the square root of time: ⟨r²⟩ = 6Dt in 3D, with D the diffusion coefficient.

The drag

Throw the grain and the liquid stops it: the same molecules that jiggle it, now hitting its front more often than its back. The friction ζ is how hard the liquid resists motion (force per unit speed). Here it is read from the throws: ζ = m / ∫ v(t)/v₀ dt, the grain's mass over how far a throw carries it, measured in time. (The speed falls fast at first, then lingers: the liquid the grain set moving pushes it along a while; that lingering counts.)

Einstein's relation, and the number of atoms

Einstein showed in 1905 that D = kT/ζ: the spreading and the drag are two faces of the same collisions, and their product is the temperature, kT = Dζ. Measured here: the readout compares it with the thermometer. For a sphere in water ζ = 6πηa (Stokes), so watching a grain of known radius a in water of known viscosity η gives k = Dζ/T; and since the gas constant R = N k was known from gases, N = R/k: Avogadro's number. Jean Perrin did exactly this from 1908, tracking grains of gamboge and mastic, and got N ≈ 6–7 × 10²³, matching a dozen other methods; it won him the 1926 Nobel prize and ended the argument over whether atoms are real.

Caveats

  • The grain's D creeps up the longer you watch (0.010 over 5 τ, 0.017 over 20 τ, 0.022 over 80 τ): the liquid's own swirls carry it, the hydrodynamic "long-time tail", and the periodic box shapes them. The relation holds when D and ζ are measured over the same window (here, 20 τ for D and the first 4 τ of the throws, where the throws' speed has died away; measured natively: D 0.0169, ζ 59, Dζ = 1.0).
  • A grain only five molecules across: Perrin's were thousands of times bigger, and the liquid was a continuum to them.
  • No heat bath: the whole simulation conserves its energy.
