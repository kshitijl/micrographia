Water moves toward the salt

Water molecules cross the membrane in both directions all the time. Salt cannot cross, and where there is more salt, a little less of the volume is water, so slightly fewer water molecules hit the membrane from that side. The net flow is from the less salty side to the saltier side. This is osmosis (see where the push comes from): the salt trapped inside the cell pushes outward on the membrane like a gas, with the osmotic pressure Π = c kT.

  • Pure water outside: nothing outside balances the salt inside, so water flows in and the cells swell, turning red as they near their limit, then burst: the membrane tears and the salt spills out. A real red blood cell has some spare membrane (it is a flattened disc), so it can swell into a sphere; beyond that its membrane can barely stretch (it tears at about 3% strain), and the cell bursts. That is haemolysis, and an IV of pure water would destroy blood cells by the billion, releasing their haemoglobin into the blood.
  • Saline outside: 0.9% salt solution matches the salt inside the cells (it is “isotonic”), so the flows balance and the cell keeps its size.
  • Brine outside: water is drawn out, and the cells shrink, wrinkle and turn blue. Real red cells crenate: they shrivel into spiky balls that are stiff and can no longer squeeze through capillaries narrower than themselves, so blood stops flowing where it needs to. Brain cells shrinking in very salty blood can tear the small blood vessels around them. Very salty drinks dehydrate you for the same reason.

Why the big cell tears

The second panel's cell is one ring of 154 beads, 35 σ across, joined by springs about seven times stiffer than the small cells' and with no spare membrane: it starts as round as it can be. Water cannot swell it, so it pushes on it instead. The salt inside presses outward with its osmotic pressure, and a round membrane holds that pressure by tension, pulling along itself like the skin of a balloon: tension = pressure × radius (Laplace's law, in 2D). The springs barely lengthen, but every one of them pulls a little harder, and the bigger the cell, the harder the pull for the same salt. When the tension, averaged over 20 τ, passes its limit, the membrane tears at its most stretched spring and the salt spills out.

Even in saline the tension isn't zero: the ring's thermal wriggling pulls the springs slightly longer on average. That baseline is what the colours call “as in saline”; pure water adds the osmotic pull on top, and brine takes it away (the cell crumples a little instead).

Caveats

  • 2D, and the membrane is a ring of beads joined by springs, with springs between next-nearest beads for stiffness, and gaps that pass small water molecules and block larger salt particles. Real membranes pass water through dedicated channels (aquaporins).
  • The springs are stretchy, and thermal jiggling shakes them far more than osmosis stretches them, so the model cannot burst by a spring snapping. Instead it follows the real rule: a cell bursts once it has swollen to its limit (an area, averaged over 10 τ, of 272 σ², where cells in saline settle at about 210); the membrane then tears where it is most stretched.
  • Even in saline the ring's thermal wiggling makes its area fluctuate; compare averages.
  • The big cell has no heat bath: its energy is constant (the small cells sit in a gentle bath). Tearing removes three springs and the little energy they held.
  • Ions are uncharged particles here; real salt dissociates into two ions, which doubles its osmotic effect.
