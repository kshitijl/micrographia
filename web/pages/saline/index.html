Osmosis in the body

Why are IV drips saline? What if they were pure water, or much saltier?

A model red blood cell: a flexible membrane (a ring of linked beads) that water molecules can slip through but salt cannot. Inside is salty fluid. Put the cell in pure water, in matching saline, or in brine, and watch which way water moves.

Water moves toward the salt

Water molecules cross the membrane in both directions all the time. Salt cannot cross, and where there is more salt, a little less of the volume is water, so slightly fewer water molecules hit the membrane from that side. The net flow is from the less salty side to the saltier side. This is osmosis (see where the push comes from): the salt trapped inside the cell pushes outward on the membrane like a gas, with the osmotic pressure Π = c kT.

  • Pure water outside: nothing outside balances the salt inside, so water flows in and the cell swells until its stretched membrane pushes back. A real red blood cell's membrane can barely stretch, so in pure water it swells and bursts (haemolysis). An IV of pure water would destroy blood cells.
  • Saline outside: 0.9% salt solution matches the salt inside the cells (it is “isotonic”), so the flows balance and the cell keeps its size.
  • Brine outside: water flows out, and the cell shrinks and crinkles (crenation). Very salty drinks dehydrate you for the same reason.

Caveats

  • 2D, and the membrane is a ring of beads joined by springs with gaps that pass small water molecules and block larger salt particles. Real membranes pass water through dedicated channels (aquaporins) and are nearly unstretchable, so real cells burst where this one only swells.
  • Even in saline the ring's thermal wiggling makes its area fluctuate; compare averages.
  • Ions are uncharged particles here; real salt dissociates into two ions, which doubles its osmotic effect.
