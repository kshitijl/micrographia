Micrographia
The evidence for atoms: the atom of electricity

How do we know electric charge comes in lumps?

A tiny drop of oil, a micron across, floats in the air between two metal plates, seen through a microscope. Gravity pulls it down; an electric field pushes on whatever charge it carries. Time its fall with the field off and its rise with the field on, and you can work out its charge. Do it for many drops, or knock a drop's charge with a burst of X-rays, and the charges you find are never just anything.

Timing a drop

A drop this small falls at a steady speed almost at once: the air's drag 6πηav balances its weight (4/3)πa³ρg (Stokes' law), so the falling speed gives its radius a. With the field E on, the electric force qE lifts it, and it rises at the speed where qE = weight + drag. Together: q = 6πηa (vfall + vrise) / E. Millikan timed thousands of falls and rises, from 1909 to 1913, often of one drop for hours, as X-rays or radioactivity knocked its charge up and down.

The lumps

Every charge he found was a whole multiple of one amount, 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs: the charge of one electron, e. A drop's charge jumped only by whole e's, never by halves. Dividing the faraday (the charge carried by a mole of singly-charged ions in electrolysis, 96,500 coulombs) by e gives Avogadro's number again, 6 × 10²³: another of Perrin's independent counts, this one from electricity.

The jiggle

The drop does not fall smoothly: it is small enough to be kicked about by air molecules, the same Brownian motion as pollen in water. It blurs each timing a little, which is why many timings were needed; Millikan noted it, and the jiggle itself was another way to measure the Boltzmann constant.

Caveats

  • Not molecules: one drop, moved by gravity, the field, Stokes drag and random Brownian kicks (Langevin dynamics), in real units: oil of density 920 kg/m³ in air of viscosity 1.82 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s at 20 °C, plates 8 mm apart.
  • Millikan also corrected Stokes' law for drops so small that air is not quite a continuum (the Cunningham correction); this simulation leaves it out, so its formula is exact here.
  • The drop's charge is drawn at random (1 to 8 e); an X-ray burst changes it by one or two e, either way.

About this simulation

  • Method: Langevin (Brownian) dynamics of one oil drop: each step moves it by the forces (gravity, buoyancy, the field) over its Stokes friction 6πηa, times Δt, plus a random step √(2D Δt), with D = kT / 6πηa.
  • Ensemble: the air is a heat bath at 20 °C (the random kicks and the friction balance at kT); the drop itself has no inertia at these sizes.
  • Size: one drop in real units (oil 920 kg/m³, air viscosity 1.82 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s, plates 8 mm apart); time step 2 ms.
