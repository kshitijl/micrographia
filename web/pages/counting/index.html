The evidence for atoms: radioactivity

How did anyone count atoms one at a time?

A radioactive atom gives no warning: at some moment, at random, its nucleus throws out an α particle, which makes a tiny flash where it hits a zinc sulphide screen. In a dark room, with a magnifying glass, you can see them, and count them: each flash is one atom. Rutherford and Geiger did this in 1908, and from counting flashes and catching the helium the α particles became, the number of atoms in a gram.

Press Run. A tenth of the decays send their α particle into the screen. The counts in equal intervals jump about at random, but in a pattern: Poisson's. And as the atoms are used up, the flashes thin out, halving every half-life.

Randomness with a pattern

Each atom has the same small chance of decaying in each second, whatever its neighbours do and however long it has waited. So the number of flashes in an interval is random, but random in a definite way: if the average is μ, the chance of k is μke−μ/k! (Poisson's distribution), and the spread around the average is √μ. Rutherford and Geiger checked this in 1910 by counting flashes in thousands of eighth-of-a-minute intervals. That the counts follow it is evidence that each flash is a separate, independent event: one atom.

The half-life

With the same chance per atom per second, a fixed fraction of whatever is left decays each second: the number left, and the flash rate with it, falls exponentially, halving every half-life. The readout fits it from your counts.

Counting a gram

A gram of radium gives off about 3.4 × 10¹⁰ α particles a second (Rutherford and Geiger counted them, with a screen and later with the first electrical counter, Geiger's). The α particles pick up electrons and become helium atoms, and Rutherford and Royds showed in 1909 that they really are helium, by collecting it. Measuring the volume of helium a known amount of radium makes in a year, and dividing it into the number of α particles counted, gives the number of atoms in a volume of gas, and so N: Perrin's table lists about 6 × 10²³ from this, again.

Caveats

  • Not molecular dynamics: each atom decays at random with the probability set by the half-life (a Monte Carlo of independent atoms), and a fixed tenth of the decays light the screen.
  • A made-up isotope with a short half-life, so that the decay shows in minutes; radium's is 1,600 years.

About this simulation

  • Method: not molecules but a Monte Carlo of independent atoms: in each sixtieth of a second, the number that decay is drawn from the Poisson distribution with mean (atoms left) × λ × Δt, and a tenth of them light the screen.
  • Energy: each decay releases its α particle's energy, which leaves the system; no ensemble, as decay is not an equilibrium.
  • Size: the number of atoms you put in; time step 1/60 s.
