Probability and entropy

Could all the air in your room rush into one corner? How long would you wait?

Nothing in the laws of motion stops every air molecule from wandering into the same half of the room at once. So instead of one room, here are 768 tiny boxes of gas, each with a few molecules, all moving at once on your GPU. A box lights up whenever all of its molecules happen to be in its left half.

What you're seeing

Each little box is a separate container of gas with hard walls. The molecules bounce off each other and the walls, and they never leave their box. The bar chart counts, across all boxes, how many molecules are in the left half right now. Each molecule is equally likely to be on either side, independently of the others, so the counts follow the binomial distribution: the even splits are common and the lopsided ones rare.

The odds

For one molecule the chance of being on the left is ½. For all N of them at once it is ½ × ½ × … = 2−N. With 6 molecules that is 1 in 64, so a dozen boxes light up at any moment. With 16 molecules it is 1 in 65,536, and you will rarely see one in the whole grid. Each molecule you add halves the odds again.

Why bigger molecules gather less often

2−N assumes the molecules are points, each free to be anywhere. Real molecules take up room: no two can overlap, so each one denies the others a little area around itself. Squeezed into half the box, they deny each other twice as large a share of the space, so the gathered arrangements are rarer still. For N molecules in a box of area A, each denying an area b to every other, the odds shrink by a factor of about e−N(N−1)b/2A. With tiny molecules (0.25 σ in an 8 σ box) the correction is a few percent and the gas behaves ideally, as air at room conditions does (its molecules fill a thousandth of the volume). With 1 σ molecules it halves the odds. The page computes b from the molecules' actual repulsion and shows both predictions; the size correction is only the first term of a series, so it slightly overestimates the odds for large, crowded molecules.

How long would you wait in your room?

The arrangement of molecules reshuffles every time they cross the box, so you get a fresh chance roughly every crossing time. For a room that is about the time sound takes to cross it, a few hundredths of a second. A room holds roughly 1027 molecules, so the odds per chance are 2−1027: a number with about 3 × 1026 zeros after the decimal point. The age of the universe is about 4 × 1017 seconds. No amount of waiting comes remotely close. The air spreads out not because a law forbids it from gathering, but because spread-out arrangements outnumber gathered ones by a factor beyond imagination. That counting is what entropy measures.

Caveats

  • The molecules are discs in 2D, drawn at least half a σ across so they can be seen (the tiny ones are smaller than drawn).
  • Snapshots taken close together are not independent, so the measured share fluctuates more than the number of snapshots suggests.
