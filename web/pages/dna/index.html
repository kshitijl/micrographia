COVID, vaccines, public health

How does a PCR test copy DNA? Why do its strands come apart when heated, and stay together when cool?

A PCR machine cycles a sample between about 95 °C, where the two strands of DNA separate, and about 55 °C, where short primers stick to them so the copying can start. That only works because strands pair and unpair at well-defined temperatures that depend on their length. Here each box holds one pair of model strands; the rows hold short, medium and long ones. Many weak bonds make a strong one

A single pair of beads is held by one attraction, worth a few kT. Thermal kicks break it easily, and once broken the two beads wander off: at these temperatures a lone pair spends little time together. A strand is different. Once a few of its beads are paired, the rest are held right next to their partners and pair at almost no further cost in freedom. Separating the strands means breaking all the pairs at once, so the strands stay together up to a melting temperature that rises with their length. Near it, the histogram shows strands either fully apart or mostly zipped, with frayed ends that open and close; half-paired strands are rarer.

Real DNA melts more sharply than this model, because neighbouring base pairs also stack on each other, so a pair is much more stable next to an existing one. The model has only the zipping.

PCR exploits this. At 95 °C every double strand of the sample melts. At 55 °C the primers, about 20 bases long, are cold enough to pair firmly with their targets, long enough to be specific, and short enough to melt off places where they match only partly. Chemists choose primer lengths by their melting temperatures. At 72 °C the polymerase extends them into full copies, and the cycle doubles the number of copies of the target, about thirty times over in a COVID test.

The model: beads in a strand are joined by springs and stiffened by springs between next-nearest beads; A beads attract only B beads. Real DNA pairs specific bases (A with T, G with C), stacks them, and is charged; none of that is here, only the statistical mechanics of zipping.
