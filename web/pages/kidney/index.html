Osmosis at work

How do kidneys make urine four times saltier than blood?

Water only ever moves by osmosis, from less salty to more salty, and no cell membrane can pump it. Yet a kidney sends out urine at up to 1,200 milliosmoles per litre, four times blood's 300. The trick is a hairpin: a tube that goes down into the kidney and comes back up beside itself, with salt pumped across between the two arms. Each pump manages only a small step; the hairpin multiplies them.

The hairpin

The loop of Henle runs down from the kidney's outer layer (the cortex) deep into the medulla, turns, and runs back up beside itself. The ascending arm's wall pumps salt out into the tissue around it, using ATP, but lets no water follow; each stretch of pump can make the tissue only about 200 mOsm saltier than the fluid inside. The descending arm is the opposite: water can leave but salt cannot, so as its fluid flows down past the salty tissue it loses water by osmosis and gets saltier, matching the tissue around it.

That saltier fluid turns the corner and comes up the ascending arm, where the pumps push salt out of it, 200 mOsm above what is inside: the tissue at that depth ends up saltier than before; so the next fluid coming down loses more water there, arrives at the corner saltier still, and so on. Because the two arms flow in opposite directions (countercurrent), a small step across, at every depth, adds up to a big difference from top to bottom: the multiplier. Watch it build from all-300 at the start.

The urine

The urine itself is made in the collecting duct, which runs down through the same salty medulla. With the hormone ADH (vasopressin) present, its wall has water channels (aquaporins), and water leaves it by osmosis into the salty tissue, so the urine leaves as salty as the deepest tissue: concentrated, a little water spent. Without ADH the duct is watertight and the urine stays dilute: what happens after a lot of drinking, or with alcohol, which suppresses ADH.

Where the energy goes

Nothing here moves water with energy: all the water moves by osmosis, downhill. The energy is spent on moving salt uphill, against its gradient; the order (low entropy) of the concentrated medulla is paid for by ATP. A longer loop multiplies more, as long as the blood flowing through the medulla does not wash the salt away as fast: desert rodents such as the kangaroo rat have very long loops and little medullary blood flow, make urine at over 5,000 mOsm/L, and need almost no water to drink. (Try a long loop with the blood flow turned right down.)

Caveats

  • Not molecules: the textbook stepwise model of the multiplier, depth by depth (after Guyton's and Koeppen and Stanton's diagrams): at each depth the pumps split the ascending fluid and the descending fluid with its tissue about their mean, 200 mOsm apart, then the fluid moves on a step. It tracks saltiness, not volumes; the ascending arm's last stretch, in the cortex, is taken to bring its fluid to 200 below the cortex's 300.
  • The real inner medulla works partly differently: urea, recycled from the collecting duct, makes up much of its saltiness, and the thin ascending arm there has no pumps.
  • The tissue's blood vessels (the vasa recta) also loop, which limits how much they wash away; the slider sets how much they do.
