Bouncing off a moving wall

A molecule that hits a piston moving toward it comes back faster, the way a tennis ball comes off a swinging racket faster than it arrived. A molecule hitting a retreating piston comes back slower. Compressing a gas means every bounce adds a little speed, so the temperature rises; expanding it means every bounce takes some away. The energy comes from whoever pushes the piston: the work done on the gas shows up, collision by collision, as faster molecules.

Shocks

Press Slam in and the piston drives down at 4 σ/τ, faster than sound travels in this gas (about 1.4 σ/τ). The molecules ahead of it cannot get out of the way in time, so they pile up in a thin, dense, hot layer, a shock wave, that runs down the cylinder ahead of the piston: watch the step in the density and temperature profile travel toward the floor, bounce, and come back. It is far from equilibrium: different parts of the gas have very different temperatures, and the gas ends up hotter than the slow-compression adiabat for the same squeeze, because shocks make entropy. Yank out pulls the piston away faster than the molecules can follow: a thin, cold gap opens under it, which the gas fills only as fast as its molecules move, and the work the gas does on the piston is less than in a slow expansion.

The adiabat

With insulating walls no heat can leave, so all the work stays in the gas. For a slow compression of this two-dimensional gas the temperature then rises exactly as the area falls, T·A = constant (in 3D it is T·V2/3 for a monatomic gas, and T·V0.4 for air). Halve the area and the temperature doubles. Compress fast, faster than the molecules move, and the gas heats even more: the piston piles molecules up in front of it, and the extra work is not recovered when it stops. Turn on heat-conducting walls and compress slowly: heat flows out as fast as work comes in, and the temperature barely moves (isothermal).

Bike pumps and spray cans

A bike pump compresses air quickly in a tube that conducts heat poorly, so the air (and then the pump) gets hot; friction in the pump adds a little. A diesel engine compresses air about twenty times, heating it to several hundred degrees, hot enough to ignite the injected fuel. The cold of a spray can is mostly something else: its propellant is a liquid that boils as it leaves, and evaporation takes heat from the can. Expansion of the gas itself also cools it, as this simulation shows when you expand.
