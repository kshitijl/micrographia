The depletion force (Asakura and Oosawa, 1954)

Can a crowd push two things together, with no attraction at all?

Two big disks sit in a crowd of small ones. Nothing attracts anything: every collision is a pure push. Yet the big disks are pushed together, and held together. It is the crowd's entropy: when the big ones touch, there is more room for the small ones. This is how the inside of a cell, packed with proteins, presses molecules together, and why adding a polymer makes paint or milk proteins clump.

Run, and watch the push build up (it is the average of thousands of tiny knocks; give it a minute). The big disks are held still, half a molecule apart. Pull them apart past one small disk's width and the push goes. Heat the crowd and it pushes harder. Then let go of them.

Where the push comes from

A small disk's centre cannot come closer to a big one than the sum of their radii: around each big disk there is a zone it can't enter (drawn here as a ring). When the big disks are close, their zones overlap, and the small disks lose less room in all: the overlap is room they get back. More room is more ways to arrange themselves, more entropy, so the crowd's free energy is lower with the big disks together, and the difference is a force. Seen as collisions: in the narrow gap between the big disks there are few small ones knocking them apart, while the crowd outside knocks them together at full pressure. The force is the crowd's pressure, n kT, times the width of the overlap: Asakura and Oosawa (1954). Measured with a crowd of 0.3 per σ², at a gap of 0.3 σ: 1.01 ± 0.07 ε/σ, against their 0.90; this page's crowd is four times as dense, and pushes four times as hard (3.9 against 3.6).

Let go, the big disks stay stuck together for a while, wander apart, and find each other again: they spend about a quarter of their time touching, where at random they would touch 1.5% of the time. The well is a few kT deep, so thermal knocks can still pull them apart.

An entropic force gets stronger with heat

The push is the crowd's pressure, nkT, so at a fixed arrangement it grows in proportion to the temperature, the mark of a force made by entropy, not energy (like a rubber band's pull). A glue would do the opposite: it holds best when cold, and lets go when shaken hard. (These small disks are a little soft: hot ones squash closer to the big ones, which shrinks the overlap a little, so here the push grows somewhat less than in proportion: 1.33 ± 0.16 at twice the temperature, against 1.49 predicted with that squashing.)

Caveats

  • The small disks pass through one another (an ideal gas, as Asakura and Oosawa assumed); real crowders also push on each other, which makes the force stronger and gives it ripples.
  • The push is added up by the page from the small disks' forces on the held big ones (each knock exactly as the simulation computes it), and averaged while you hold them still; it is noisy, and settles over a minute or so.
  • While held, the big disks are pinned; moving them does work on the crowd. Let go, they become free disks with the mass of their area.
