Einstein, 1905: special relativity, and E = mc²

Can a gas get hotter without its molecules getting faster? And is a hot cup of tea heavier?

Nothing goes faster than light. So what happens when you keep heating a gas whose molecules are already nearly that fast? They can't speed up much, but they keep taking the heat. Where does it go? Into their mass. This gas lives in a world where light is slow (it crosses a molecule in one time unit), so you can see it happen.

Hold the button: every molecule in both gases gets the same steady push. The hot gas falls behind: it is harder to get moving. Its inertia (the momentum given ÷ the speed its molecules have gained) comes out as its energy plus its pressure × area, over c²: heat included. Heat it more and it gets heavier still.

Hotter but not faster

In Newton's mechanics a molecule's energy is ½mv², so a hotter gas has faster molecules without limit. In Einstein's (1905) it is E = γmc², with γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²), which grows without limit as v approaches c while v itself never gets there. So heating a gas whose molecules are near c mostly makes them heavier, not faster: the energy goes into γ. Their speeds pile up just below c, in the distribution Jüttner worked out in 1911, and the gas takes twice as much heat per degree as it did when cold (in 2D: from 1k per molecule to 2k).

How do you read a temperature off such a gas? Not from ½mv². The general form of equipartition says that each direction of motion carries ⟨p·v⟩/2 = ½kT on average, whatever the relation between energy and momentum, so the thermometer here is kT = ⟨p·v⟩/2. Its readings agree with Jüttner's formula for the energy.

Is a hot cup of tea heavier?

Yes. Push the two gases alike and the hot one gains speed more slowly: its molecules' heat has inertia, as their rest mass does. E = mc², read the other way: m = E/c². In Einstein's September 1905 paper the example was a body giving off light, which gets lighter by the light's energy over c². A cup of tea 80 degrees hotter (330 joules more per gram) is heavier by about four millionths of a microgram per gram: real, and far too small to weigh.

Measured here, the gases' inertia per molecule is not quite E/c² but (E + pA)/c², energy plus pressure times area: the enthalpy, which turned up on another page for a different reason. These gases have no walls (the box is periodic), and a gas on its own, squeezed by its surroundings, moves its pressure's energy along with it: in relativity a fluid's inertia per volume is (ε + p)/c². Put the gas in a closed box and the box's walls, stretched by that pressure, carry −pA/c² of inertia, so the whole box weighs exactly its total energy over c² (Laue, 1911). For tea, pV is a few thousandths of the heat, and the difference does not matter.

Caveats

  • Hard disks that collide at contact: the collision rule (reverse the momenta along the line of centres, in the pair's centre-of-momentum frame) conserves energy and momentum exactly, but a rigid disk is not itself a relativistic object, and contact is judged at equal times in the lab frame.
  • The push acts on every molecule alike (like a uniform field), so no walls come into it; see above for what walls would change. The inertia is fitted from how far the molecules have flowed on average against the momentum given; the mean flow jiggles, so the reading settles only after a long push (the test averages eight pushes of 120 time units: 1.03 against 1.025 cold, 3.09 against 3.07 hot).
  • 2D, and a slow light, so that the effects are large; how temperature changes between moving frames is still argued over, and this page does not need an answer.

About this simulation

  • Method: relativistic hard-disk dynamics in 2D (JavaScript), in units with c = 1 and the molecules' rest mass m = 1: each step moves every disk by its velocity p/E, then disks that touch and are approaching collide. A periodic box (no walls).
  • Ensemble: microcanonical. Collisions conserve total energy and momentum exactly; the energy changes only when you heat or cool (which scales every momentum by the same factor) or push.
  • Size: 600 disks of diameter 1 in a 50 × 50 box (part 1); 300 in a 35 × 35 box each (part 2). Time step 0.02 (a disk crosses at most 2% of its diameter per step).
