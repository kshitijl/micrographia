The atmosphere

Why does air pressure fall with height? And is it really colder up high?

A column of gas under gravity: light molecules and heavier ones, bouncing off each other and off the floor. Gravity pulls them down; their motion spreads them up. The balance gives the shape of an atmosphere. It also gives a surprise about temperature.

Gravity, mass and temperature

The shape of the column depends on one number: m g / kT, the energy it costs to climb, compared with the energy molecules have to spend. Doubling the mass or doubling gravity does the same thing to where the molecules end up (heavier molecules only move more slowly), so the page has a gravity slider and two kinds of molecule, and a temperature slider. Turn up gravity and the air hugs the floor; heat the gas and it spreads up.

Pressure is the weight of the air above

Each layer of gas has to hold up the weight of everything above it, and it does so by pressure: molecules below push up harder than molecules above push down. Pressure in a gas is density times temperature (p = n kT), so to push harder the lower layers must be denser. Work that out layer by layer and the density falls off exponentially with height,

n(h) = n(0) e−m g h / kT

which is just the Boltzmann factor for the energy m g h it costs to be up at height h. The density chart is on a log scale, so exponentials are straight lines. Heavier molecules have a steeper line: each kind follows its own exponential, with its own “scale height” kT/(mg). On Earth that is about 8 km for air.

Why cumulus clouds have flat bases

Press "Make it humid" and then Sunshine. The ground is now moist, and the sun evaporates water into the air touching it. The warm, moist air rises in plumes, and as it rises it expands into lower pressure and cools, about 10 °C for every kilometre (the dry adiabat: the dashed guide on the temperature profile). The water in it stays invisible vapour until the air has cooled to its dew point; there it is saturated, and from there up the extra vapour condenses into droplets: cloud.

Every plume starts from the same ground, with about the same temperature and the same moisture, so every plume reaches its dew point at about the same height: the lifting condensation level, roughly 125 m for every degree between the ground air's temperature and its dew point (the dashed white line). That height is the same across the whole sky, so all the clouds start there: flat bottoms, at one level, with lumpy tops wherever each plume happened to carry its water. Condensing also gives back the water's latent heat, which warms the cloudy air and keeps it rising: why cumulus tower upward.

So why is it colder on a mountain?

Look at the temperature chart: once the column settles, the temperature is the same at every height. That is not a fluke, and nothing here forces it: the column is sealed off, with no heat bath anywhere, and its total energy never changes. In equilibrium, gravity sorts molecules by height but not by speed: the Boltzmann factor for height and the one for speed are separate, so the speed distribution, and therefore the temperature, is the same everywhere. Fewer molecules make it up high, but the ones that do are just as fast. (Loschmidt argued the opposite in the 1870s, and Boltzmann and Maxwell argued back; this is the answer.) Start the gas squeezed against the ceiling and watch it fall: the fall turns potential energy into heat, and the column settles much hotter. Start it against the floor and it cools as it spreads upward, doing work against gravity. Turn on the hot plate and the floor warms the gas from below: the lower layers heat first, and the temperature falls with height for a while. But with only a hot plate and nothing cold, the column ends up at the plate's temperature all the way up. A steady fall of temperature with height needs somewhere for the heat to go; the real atmosphere radiates it to space.

The real atmosphere is colder aloft because it is not in equilibrium. Sunlight heats the ground, the ground heats the air above it, and warm air rises. Rising air expands as the pressure drops, and a gas that expands without taking in heat cools (see the bike pump page). Convection keeps stirring the lower atmosphere, so the temperature falls by roughly 6.5 °C per kilometre: the cooling of rising, expanding air, not an effect of height itself.

Part 2 shows this with a fluid model instead of molecules. Air carries its “potential temperature” with it: the temperature it would have if brought down to the ground's pressure without gaining or losing heat. Sunshine warms the bottom; warm air rises, mixes, and the stirred layer ends up with one potential temperature throughout, which means a real temperature falling with height at g/cp ≈ 9.8 °C per km for dry air (the real 6.5 °C/km is lower because rising air condenses water, which releases heat). Above the stirred layer the air is still in its old, isothermal state, and the layer eats upward into it as the day goes on. At night the ground cools the air touching it, and cold air under warm air is stable: nothing stirs, and whatever is released near the ground stays there (smog).

Then why is a sunny plateau cold?

A high plateau is sunlit and warms the air touching it, just as the low ground does. So why does height above sea level, and not height above the local ground, predict the temperature? Three reasons, the first the biggest:

  • Less air above means less blanket. The ground's warmth is mostly set by radiation: sunlight in, infrared out. The air above the ground absorbs much of the outgoing infrared and sends some back down (the greenhouse effect), and the more air there is above you, the more of that blanket you have. A plateau at 4 km has 40% of the atmosphere below it, missing from its blanket, so it loses heat to space much faster, above all at night under clear skies.
  • The air is borrowed. Wind keeps bringing air in at that height from the open sky around the plateau, air that is cold because of the lapse rate set over the whole planet's low ground. A plateau warms its own boundary layer by day, but it is small next to the air streaming over it.
  • Thin air heats and cools easily. At low pressure there is less air per cubic metre to hold heat, so plateau air warms fast in the sun and chills fast at night: the daily swings are huge.

So the local ground does matter a little: big plateaus are measurably warmer than the open air at the same height (the “mass elevation effect”; the Tibetan plateau even heats the air above it enough to help drive the Asian monsoon). But they are much colder than sea level, because the whole atmosphere's temperature profile, set by convection over the planet as a whole, is the backdrop they sit in.

The same thing with slabs

Cut the column into slabs. The gas in each slab has to hold up its own weight plus everything above it, so the pressure at its bottom is its pressure at the top plus its weight per unit width: pbelow = pabove + n m g Δz. With p = n kT, each slab's density is the one above it times (1 + m g Δz / kT); going upward, each slab is thinner than the one below by the same factor. With N slabs over a height H that is (1 − x/N)k for slab k, where x = m g H / kT. As the slabs get thinner this becomes the differential equation dn/dz = −(m g / kT) n, whose solution is the exponential. Slide N up and watch the steps merge into the curve.

Caveats

  • 2D, small soft molecules (nearly an ideal gas). Gravity is set very strong so that a column a few hundred molecules tall shows a full atmosphere.
  • On Earth turbulence mixes the lower ~100 km, so the heavy and light gases of air do not separate as they do here; they do separate higher up.
  • The fluid panel is a quick sketch, not a weather model: two dimensions, dry air, the motion treated as incompressible (the Boussinesq approximation, a stretch over a 10 km column) while the temperature and pressure are not, a uniform “eddy” viscosity standing in for the turbulence the grid cannot resolve, a slow drag and radiation towards the start, and an absorbing layer under the lid to let waves escape as they would into the open sky. The sunshine is several times stronger than real. Two-dimensional plumes are also fatter and slower than real three-dimensional ones.
