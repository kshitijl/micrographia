How does a buffer hold blood's pH steady when acid is added?

Acidity is the concentration of free protons (H⁺). A buffer is a crowd of molecules that grab protons and let them go again. Each box here gets some acid: from none in the left column to the most in the right. The bottom rows also hold buffer molecules; the top rows are plain water. Count the free protons in each.

Soaking up protons

In plain water every proton added stays free, so doubling the acid doubles the free-proton concentration. In the buffered boxes most added protons end up sitting on a buffer anion (A⁻ + H⁺ → HA), and only a few stay free. The plot shows −log₁₀ of the free-proton concentration, the pH of this toy world: the buffered curve sits higher (less acid) and falls slowly until the buffer is used up, at the equivalence point of one proton per anion (40 per box). Past that, the extra acid has nowhere to go.

Why the anions take protons and give them back is statistical mechanics. A bound proton has lower energy (the well depth ε) but far less room to move. The balance of the two is a constant: the law of mass action, [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] = K, with K ∝ e−ε/kT. The simulation measures that ratio in every column and finds it the same while the free protons vary tenfold, and the dashed curve predicted from K alone (Henderson–Hasselbalch) runs through the measured points. Make the anion hold its proton tighter and K drops: a weaker acid, a buffer that works at a higher pH.

Blood is buffered mainly by bicarbonate: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ CO₂ + H₂O. Its pKa of 6.1 is below blood's pH of 7.4, so on its own it would be a poor buffer. It works because the lungs breathe out the CO₂, pulling the reaction to the right and taking away the added protons, while the kidneys adjust the bicarbonate. Proteins (haemoglobin above all) and phosphate add more capacity. A swing of 0.4 in blood pH is life-threatening, while the same acid in pure water would shift its pH by several units.

The model: protons and anions are discs in an implicit solvent (a heat bath). A proton binds in a short, deep Lennard-Jones well; repulsions keep it to one proton per anion and one anion per proton. Real protons hop between water molecules and the pH scale here is in model units, but the mass-action logic is exactly the chemist's.
