Same energy, different speeds

At the same temperature every molecule has the same average kinetic energy ½mv², so lighter molecules move faster: a helium atom is about 2.7 times faster than an average air molecule, because air molecules are about 7.25 times heavier (√7.25 ≈ 2.7). A molecule leaves through a hole only if it happens to arrive there, and faster molecules arrive more often. So the rate at which each gas leaks out through small holes goes as 1/√m. That is Graham's law of effusion, found by experiment in the 1840s:

rateHe / rateair = √(mair / mHe) ≈ 2.7

The page measures each gas's leak rate from how fast its count inside falls and compares the ratio with √(mass ratio). Change the heavy gas's mass and watch the ratio follow.

Real balloons

A rubber balloon has no actual holes: gas molecules dissolve into the rubber and diffuse through it. That is a different process, but it favours small, light molecules too, which is why helium balloons sag within a day while air-filled ones last for weeks. (Foil balloons hold helium much longer.) The holes here show the simplest version of the effect: pure speed.

Caveats

  • 2D, tiny molecules (σ = 0.3) in a dilute gas, so that molecules reach the holes one at a time; in a dense gas flow through a hole is collective and the mass dependence weakens.
  • Escaped molecules can come back in; the measured ratio uses the early, nearly exponential part of the leak and reads a little below √(mass ratio).
