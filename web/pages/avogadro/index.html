Why do gases combine in whole-number volumes?

A litre of hydrogen and a litre of chlorine, lit by sunlight, explode into hydrogen chloride, and when it has cooled back to room temperature and pressure there are exactly two litres of it. Not one, as Dalton's single atoms would give, and not some untidy amount. Gay-Lussac found whole numbers like this for every gas reaction he tried. Avogadro saw why: equal volumes of gases hold equal numbers of molecules, and a hydrogen molecule is two atoms.

Each cylinder starts with 1 volume of hydrogen and 1 of chlorine, mixed, under the same weighted lid and between the same room-temperature walls. In the dark nothing happens. Run, then flash a light (or hold the lamp over the left one): the lid jumps as the mixture explodes, then settles as the walls take the heat away. Compare the volumes then.

Equal volumes, equal numbers

A gas's pressure comes from its molecules hitting the walls, and at a given temperature each molecule, light or heavy, one atom or two, hits with the same average push (see the heavy molecule). So under the same lid at the same temperature, a volume holds a fixed number of molecules, whatever the gas: Avogadro's hypothesis (1811). Measured here: 300 molecules of two atoms each hold the lid at 105 σ, 300 single atoms at 103 σ (the same, within the lid's jiggle; the gas is thin enough that the bigger molecules hardly crowd each other, and real gases at room conditions agree to a fraction of a percent).

So volumes count molecules

If volumes count molecules, then gases must combine in simple ratios of volume, because molecules combine in simple ratios of number: one H with one Cl. Gay-Lussac's 1 + 1 → 2 says more: the product has as many molecules as the two gases had together, so each hydrogen molecule and each chlorine molecule must split between two molecules of HCl. They are two atoms each. Dalton, who took the simplest guess (single atoms: H + Cl → HCl), predicted 1 + 1 → 1 and refused to believe Gay-Lussac's measurements. Steam is the same story: 2 volumes of hydrogen and 1 of oxygen give 2 of steam, so oxygen splits in two as well, and water is H₂O, not Dalton's HO. It took until Cannizzaro, in 1860, for chemists to accept it.

Why light sets it off

Hydrogen and chlorine sit together quietly in the dark: two molecules that meet do nothing. Light splits a chlorine molecule (its bond is the weakest of the three) into two free atoms. A free chlorine atom takes a hydrogen atom from H₂ (HCl's bond is about as strong as H₂'s), leaving a free hydrogen atom, which takes a chlorine atom from Cl₂ (a much stronger bond than Cl₂'s, so it releases a lot of heat), leaving a free chlorine atom again: a chain, thousands of links long for each atom the light freed, until two free atoms meet and join. That is why the mixture explodes in sunlight. Watch the glowing free atoms carry it.

Caveats

  • The chemistry is a rule, not quantum mechanics: at each look (every 40 steps), atoms in contact react if the bond energies (H–H 4.52 eV, H–Cl 4.47, Cl–Cl 2.48) allow it, by making and breaking bonds (springs) in the simulation. Each atom can have one bond.
  • The heat each reaction releases is scaled down: 1 ε per eV, where really an eV is about 39 kT at room temperature. The real explosion is far more violent.
  • Chlorine is drawn twice as heavy as hydrogen (really 35 times); masses do not change a gas's volume, which is the point.
  • 2D: volumes are areas. The lid is in contact with the room as well (a little friction, with the matching random jiggle), so that its bounce dies down.
