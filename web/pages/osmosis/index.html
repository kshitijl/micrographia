Osmosis

Why is osmosis an entropic force? Where does the push actually come from?

A membrane down the middle lets solvent molecules (blue) through but not solute molecules (light). The solute starts on the right. Nothing here attracts anything: every molecule just bounces off the others. Yet the membrane feels a steady push. Here is where it comes from, measured collision by collision.

The push is solute molecules bouncing off the membrane

Solvent molecules pass through the membrane as if it were not there, so they cannot push on it. Solute molecules cannot pass: every time one reaches the membrane it bounces back, and each bounce gives the membrane a little kick toward the pure-solvent side. Add up the kicks per unit time and length and you get the osmotic pressure Π. That is all the force there is. It is the same kind of push a gas exerts on a wall, and for a dilute solute it has the same formula as an ideal gas, van 't Hoff's law:

Π = c kT

with c the solute concentration. Record a few points at different concentrations and they fall close to this line. At higher concentrations they fall below it: crowding near the membrane keeps solute away from it a little.

Why “entropic”?

No attraction or repulsion between solute and solvent is needed: turn the attraction on and the pressure barely changes. What matters is that solute molecules move around at temperature T and are confined. Double the temperature and Π doubles, just as a gas pressure does. In thermodynamic language, the solute molecules' entropy grows with the volume they can explore; the force that would let them expand is Π = T (∂S/∂V). Forces that come from entropy like this scale with temperature, unlike forces from attraction.

And the solvent?

Look at the two outer walls. The pressure on the right (solution) wall is higher than on the left (pure solvent) by about Π: the whole solution is pushing harder. Solvent has flowed in from the left until its own tendency to spread (its chemical potential) is the same on both sides; diluting the solvent with solute lowers it, so solvent moves toward the solute. If the membrane could move, it would be pushed left and the solution side would swell. That swelling is what bursts a red blood cell placed in pure water, and why IV drips are saline.

Caveats

  • 2D soft discs; the solute is the same size as the solvent. A heat bath throughout keeps the temperature fixed.
  • Pressures are time averages of noisy collisions; let them settle for a while before recording. The wall pressures include the solvent and are much larger than Π.
