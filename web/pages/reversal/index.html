Arrow of time

Why can't you un-stir cream from coffee, when every collision can run backwards?

The laws that move molecules work just as well in reverse. Film two molecules colliding, play it backwards, and nothing looks wrong. So here is a cup of “coffee” with a blob of “cream”, and a button that plays the film backwards: every velocity reversed, exactly.

What you're seeing

Each dot is a molecule: a disc that bounces off its neighbours. All of them are identical; the cream ones are only coloured differently. Run the simulation and the cream spreads, the way it does in a real cup. The “mixed” readout measures how evenly the cream is spread, from 0% for the starting blob to 100% for cream everywhere in proportion.

Press Reverse time and every molecule's velocity flips. The system then retraces its own history: the cream gathers itself back into the blob, each traced molecule's white return path lies exactly on its coloured outward one, and when the clock reaches zero the page checks every bit of every molecule's position and velocity against the start. They match exactly. Nothing in the laws of motion forbids un-stirring.

Then why doesn't it happen?

Reset, run forward again, and this time press a nudge button before reversing. It moves the five traced molecules, all the cream, or every molecule by 2−32 of a diameter, about a ten-billionth. For a while the reversed run still heads back toward the blob, and the white return paths still lie on top of the coloured outward ones. But collisions amplify small differences: each time a molecule hits another slightly off-centre, the error grows. In this fluid the error grows by a factor of about 25 per unit of time (we measure a Lyapunov exponent of 3.2 per τ). The nudged molecule's neighbourhood is completely different after about 8 τ, and the disturbance then spreads outward like a wave, reaching all 8,000 molecules by about 20 τ. Run forward for 10 τ or more before reversing, and the cream never makes it back: the white paths peel away from the coloured ones and wander off.

So un-stirring needs velocities aimed with impossible precision. Of all the arrangements the molecules could have, nearly all look mixed, and the ones that un-mix are a vanishingly thin, precisely aimed set. The tiniest disturbance moves you off that set and back among the mixed ones. That is what entropy counts, and why the arrow of time points the way it does.

How the reversal can be exact

Ordinary simulations store positions as floating-point numbers, which round slightly at every step, so running them backwards doesn't retrace the path. This one stores each position as a 64-bit integer (in units of 2−32 σ) and each velocity as an integer displacement per step. Moving a molecule is then an exact integer addition. Forces are still computed in ordinary 32-bit floats on the GPU, but each force is turned into an integer “kick” that depends only on the positions. The same positions always give the same kicks, so the reversed steps undo the forward ones bit for bit.

Caveats

  • This is a 2D fluid of thousands of identical soft discs (a WCA fluid), not coffee. A real cup has about 1025 molecules.
  • A real cup is never isolated: it trades heat with the mug and the air, which disturbs it far more than one bit ever could.
  • There is a famous demonstration (G. I. Taylor's) where dye in syrup really is un-stirred by turning the crank backwards. That works for a different reason (slow, smooth flow and very slow diffusion) and will get its own page.
