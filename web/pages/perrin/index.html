Thirteen ways to count atoms: do they agree?

Jean Perrin ended his book with a table: thirteen unrelated phenomena (the stickiness of gases, the settling of grains in water, the blue of the sky, the glow of a furnace, the flashes of radium) each turned into a count of the molecules in a gramme-molecule, Avogadro's number N, and all of them came out between 60 and 75 × 10²². Why the agreement is the evidence

Any one of these measurements could be explained away: a grain that is not quite a sphere, a law of viscosity that is only roughly right. What could not be explained away was that thirteen of them, resting on unrelated physics, gave the same number. "The real existence of the molecule is given a probability bordering on certainty", Perrin wrote, and by 1913 the last serious doubters (Ostwald among them) agreed.

He added a remark worth keeping. Eliminate N between any two of the equations and you get a relation between visible things alone: the blue of the sky predicts how fast a grain of gamboge diffuses in water. In principle one could state all such results without molecules; but laws that could only have been found by way of molecules would then lose what nourished them.

Your values

The simulations know their own N exactly: their units are mapped to the laboratory so that a perfect measurement gives 6.022 × 10²³, and the error in your value is your measurement's (and the simulation's: a small box, a short run, a formula that is only approximate). Perrin's spread, 60 to 75, came from the same kinds of thing.
