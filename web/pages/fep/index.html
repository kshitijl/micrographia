How do computers predict which drug candidate binds best?

A drug works by sticking to a pocket on a target protein. Chemists make many small variants of a molecule and want to know which sticks best. Here is a toy version: a pocket, a ligand A, and a modified ligand B. First we measure the answer the slow way, by watching them bind and unbind. Then we compute it with alchemy: turning A into B inside the computer.

Binding is a free energy

How tightly a ligand binds is measured by how much more time it spends bound than free. That ratio is a Boltzmann factor of the binding free energy, so a difference of ΔΔG between two ligands means their binding constants differ by e−ΔΔG/kT. At body temperature, 1.4 kcal/mol is a factor of 10 in potency: tiny changes in a molecule can matter enormously. Part 1 measures ΔΔG directly, by counting. It works here because the toy ligands bind and unbind every few hundred time units. Real drugs stay bound for seconds to hours, far beyond any simulation, so counting is hopeless.

The alchemical trick

Free energy depends only on start and end, not on the path between them. So instead of the physical path (ligand flying in and out of the pocket), take an unphysical one: in the computer, slowly change ligand A into ligand B while it sits in the pocket, by changing its interactions step by step (λ from 0 to 1). Do the same with the ligand in plain solvent. These two transformations close a cycle with the two binding processes:

ΔΔGbind = ΔGA→B, in pocket − ΔGA→B, in solvent

Each ΔG comes from averages over short simulations at each λ. Thermodynamic integration adds up the average slope ⟨∂U/∂λ⟩ (the chart); free-energy perturbation uses ⟨e−ΔU/kT⟩ between neighbouring windows. Neither needs a single binding event. This is the method behind commercial tools such as Schrödinger's FEP+ (built on the Desmond molecular dynamics engine), used every day to decide which molecules chemists should make next.

How well does it agree?

In our native tests the alchemical prediction and the direct count agree to about 0.2 kT (≈ 0.1 kcal/mol); repeated direct counts scatter by about the same. Part of the remaining gap is a definition: the calculation holds the ligand at the pocket's centre with a spring, while the count calls anything within 0.8 σ of the centre “bound”.

Caveats

  • 2D, one-bead ligands and a five-bead pocket in a simple Lennard-Jones solvent. Real FEP handles flexible molecules, explicit water and atoms appearing or vanishing (which needs “soft-core” potentials); here only interaction strengths change, so no soft core is needed.
  • The spring that holds each ligand in place is the same for A and B, so it cancels in the cycle.
  • Energies in units of kT; ε is the Lennard-Jones well depth.
