Haemoglobin: cooperative binding, and carbon monoxide

How does blood know where to drop its oxygen? And why is carbon monoxide so deadly?

Each haemoglobin molecule carries four oxygen molecules, and the four help each other: once one is on, the rest grip harder; once one leaves, the rest let go more easily. So blood loads up fully in the lungs and unloads a big share in the tissues, over a small difference in oxygen pressure. Carbon monoxide grips 220 times harder than oxygen: a whiff of it takes seats, and it also makes the remaining oxygen cling instead of being delivered.

Run: the molecules fill with oxygen in the lungs and give a quarter of it up in the body. Exercise (the muscles' oxygen drops): they give up far more, for the same lungs. Climb a mountain. Then let in some carbon monoxide: 100 parts per million, a badly vented heater's worth.

Teamwork: the S-shaped curve

A haemoglobin molecule has two shapes: a tense one (T) that grips oxygen weakly and a relaxed one (R) that grips it a hundred times harder. Empty, it is almost always tense; each oxygen that binds tips the balance towards relaxed, which makes the other three sites grip harder (Monod, Wyman and Changeux, 1965). So the fraction carrying oxygen rises steeply, in an S, between the body's 20–40 mmHg and the lungs' 100: it is nearly full leaving the lungs and gives up a large share where the oxygen pressure is low. Myoglobin, the single-site store in muscle, has no teamwork: its curve is a plain hyperbola, full long before 20 mmHg, good for holding oxygen and bad for delivering it.

Why a little CO does so much

Carbon monoxide binds the same sites, with a free energy about 3.3 kcal/mol (5.4 kT at body temperature) more favourable than oxygen, and e5.4 ≈ 220: a Boltzmann factor. So in air with 1/2,000 as much CO as oxygen (100 ppm against 21%), CO wins about one seat in eight (Haldane: CO seats / O₂ seats = 220 × pCO / pO₂; measured here: 13%, and the oxygen delivered per trip falls by 30%). Worse, a CO on one site tips the molecule towards the relaxed, gripping shape, so the oxygen still on board clings in the tissues instead of being let go: the curve shifts left, and the delivery falls further than the seats lost. Pure oxygen (or a hyperbaric chamber) is the treatment: crowding the sites with oxygen pushes the CO off faster.

Caveats

  • Not molecules in water: a Monte Carlo of the MWC model's states (each site empty, O₂ or CO; each molecule T or R), each molecule in equilibrium with the oxygen pressure where it is; real binding takes milliseconds and the build up of CO in the blood hours.
  • Parameters: L = 1,000 (T over R, empty), dissociation constants 5 mmHg (R) and 500 mmHg (T), CO 220 times tighter; giving P50 = 27 mmHg and a Hill coefficient of 2.8, as for human blood. The Bohr effect (acid and CO₂ loosening the grip in the tissues) is left out.
  • The body's oxygen pressure is set (40 mmHg at rest, 20 exercising, less at altitude), rather than following from the delivery.
  • CO comes and goes only in the lungs, and twenty times more slowly than oxygen here (really far more slowly, over hours), so a molecule keeps its CO round the body, as real blood does.

About this simulation

  • Method: Monte Carlo (Metropolis, JavaScript) of 300 haemoglobin molecules: each step offers each site a change of occupant and each molecule a change of shape, accepted by the ratio of their Boltzmann weights.
  • Ensemble: grand canonical for the ligands: each molecule exchanges O₂ and CO with a reservoir at the local partial pressures (lungs or body), at body temperature.
