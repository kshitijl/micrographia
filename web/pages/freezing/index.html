Why does mixing salt into water lower its melting point?

A crystal lies on the floor of a box, under its own liquid, held just below its melting point: slowly the liquid freezes onto it. Pour in some "salt", atoms a little too big to fit into the crystal, and the freezing stops, or the crystal melts back, at the same temperature. Nothing about the forces between the solvent's atoms has changed. What changed is entropy.

Why salt stops the freezing

At the melting point a crystal and its liquid are in balance: atoms leave the crystal for the liquid as often as they join it from the liquid. Joining the crystal gives out heat (the crystal's bonds are lower in energy) but costs entropy (the liquid's atoms can be arranged in far more ways). Below the melting point the heat wins, above it the entropy does.

Salt does not fit into the crystal, so it stays in the liquid, and it gives the liquid something extra: the entropy of mixing, all the ways of arranging salt among solvent. Every solvent atom that freezes leaves the salt fewer solvent atoms to mix with, so it gives up a little of that mixing entropy too. Freezing now costs more entropy than before, and only a colder liquid (where heat counts for more against entropy) freezes. For a dilute solution the shift is ΔT ≈ x kTm2/L, where x is the share of salt and L the latent heat per atom: it depends only on how many salt particles there are, not what they are (a "colligative" property, like osmotic pressure, and for the same reason).

You can see the balance shift as it freezes: the crystal pushes the salt ahead of it into the remaining liquid, which gets saltier, so its freezing point falls further, until it reaches the temperature you set and the freezing stops, part way. Measured natively at kT 0.52, a tenth below the melting point, the pure liquid froze almost completely (94% of the solvent in the crystal), while with 15% salt the crystal stopped at about half (53%).

Road salt, and why it stops working in the cold

Spread salt on ice and the ice melts, though nothing is warmer: the salty water that forms at the surface has a lower melting point than the air's temperature, so the ice keeps dissolving into it, taking its heat (the latent heat) from the surroundings. But there is a limit: the more salt, the lower the melting point, until the salt itself would rather crystallise out. For ordinary salt in water that limit, the eutectic, is about −21 °C; colder than that, salt cannot keep ice melted.

Why salt changes the melting point much more than the boiling point

The same argument raises the boiling point (the vapour holds no salt, so evaporating also loses mixing entropy), by ΔT ≈ x kTb2/Lvap. For water the shifts per mole of particles per kg are 1.86 °C down for freezing and 0.51 °C up for boiling: although Tb2 is larger, the latent heat of boiling is almost seven times that of melting, and it sits in the denominator.

Caveats

  • A Lennard-Jones crystal, not ice, and "salt" atoms that are simply a little too big to fit, and cling to the solvent a little more than it clings to itself (so they stay in the liquid): real salt splits into ions that water molecules cling to. The colligative argument does not care.
  • A heat bath holds the temperature (this experiment is about temperature). The total energy is therefore not conserved: the bath removes the latent heat as the liquid freezes.
  • The crystal's floor is a flat wall, which helps hold it; its top face is the (100) face.
  • A few thousand atoms: the crystal's edge with the liquid is a large share of it, and growth and melting take tens to hundreds of τ.
