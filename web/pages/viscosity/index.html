The evidence for atoms: Maxwell, 1860; Loschmidt, 1865

Why is a gas just as sticky whatever its pressure? And how did that measure the size of a molecule?

Two gases of the same molecules, one four times denser than the other. Stir them both the same way, and watch the stirring die away. Common sense says the denser gas should be stickier: more molecules to rub against. Maxwell worked out in 1860 that it should not be, measured it, and was right.

The stirring is bands of gas moving left and right, too gentle to see among the molecules' thermal jiggling, but plain in the profile above: each gas's average sideways speed at each height, a wave that flattens as the stirring dies. Each gas's swirl dies away at its own rate; the viscosity, worked out from that rate and the gas's density, comes out the same for both.

Maxwell's surprise

Viscosity is a gas carrying sideways momentum from fast-moving layers to slow ones: molecules wander from one layer to the next, bringing their layer's motion with them. A denser gas has more molecules to carry momentum, but each one gets less far before it collides and hands it on, its mean free path shorter in proportion. The two cancel: the viscosity η ≈ ½ n m v̄ λ (in 2D) does not depend on the density n, because λ ∝ 1/n. Maxwell predicted this in 1860, found it so surprising that he measured it himself, with discs swinging in a gas at different pressures, and it held, from a sixtieth of an atmosphere to one. It was one of the first big successes of the idea that a gas is molecules flying about.

Here, the thinner gas's swirl dies away just as fast in this pair of boxes. Its viscosity is the same, but its mass per area is a quarter as big, so the same viscous force slows it four times as fast; its box is twice as wide (it has the same number of molecules), so its swirl is four times as wide, which slows the decay by four. The viscosity, worked out from each, agrees.

The size of a molecule

The mean free path is λ ≈ 2η/(n m v̄), from the measured viscosity. And λ depends on the molecules' size d: a molecule sweeps out a strip 2d wide as it flies, and travels until that strip has met another molecule, λ = 1/(2√2 n d) in 2D (in 3D, 1/(√2 n π d²)). So the viscosity gives d. Loschmidt did this in 1865 for air, getting a molecule a millionth of a millimetre across, and, from how many such molecules fill a cubic centimetre of liquid air, the first estimate of the number of molecules in a gas: Loschmidt's number, Avogadro's number's first appearance. The readout makes the same rough estimate here; it lands near the molecules' real size, within the slack of the simple formulas.

Caveats

  • 2D: discs, and the simplest kinetic-theory formulas (factors of order one are not trusted).
  • The stirring is a gentle shear wave (sideways speed up to 0.4 σ/τ, against thermal speeds of ~1), so the gas stays near equilibrium; the friction turns it into heat, warming each gas a little.
  • No heat bath: each gas conserves its energy apart from the stirring.
  • Both gases are thin enough for kinetic theory (mean free paths of about 12 and 3 σ); a liquid's viscosity works differently and does depend strongly on density.
