Entropic forces

Why does a stretched rubber band pull back, and why does it get warm?

Rubber is made of long, floppy molecules. Each chain here is a string of beads held between two pinned ends, jostled by heat. Nothing in the chain wants to be short, yet it pulls on its ends. Measure the pull, change the temperature, and find out where the force comes from.

The force is entropy

A floppy chain can wiggle into an enormous number of shapes. Most of them are crumpled; only a few are nearly straight. Holding the ends apart rules out the crumpled shapes, and the chain's constant thermal jiggling keeps tugging the ends toward the arrangements it can explore more freely. The bonds themselves do not get much longer: the pull comes from the number of shapes, that is, from entropy.

The test is temperature. An ordinary spring's pull comes from stretched bonds and hardly depends on temperature. An entropic spring's pull is the temperature times how fast the number of shapes falls as you stretch, f = −T ∂S/∂x, so it grows in proportion to T. Record the pull at a few temperatures: the points fall on a line through the origin. That is also why a rubber band holding a weight shortens when you heat it, the opposite of most materials.

Why stretching warms it

Press Stretch fast: the heat bath is switched off and the right-hand ends are pulled away quickly. Straightening the chains removes shape entropy; with no heat bath to absorb the difference, that entropy has to go somewhere, and it goes into faster jiggling of the beads. The temperature rises. Touch a rubber band to your lips, stretch it quickly, and you feel this. Let it relax and it cools again.

Caveats

  • Real rubber is a cross-linked network of chains in 3D; here each chain is separate and lives in 2D, where a chain cannot cross itself. The entropic argument is the same.
  • The beads are bonded by stiff springs (the Kremer–Grest model), so part of the pull is energetic: above T ≈ 1 the pull divided by T is constant to a few percent, but at T = 0.5 it is a third lower.
  • The surrounding solvent is not simulated; a heat bath acting on every bead stands in for it.
