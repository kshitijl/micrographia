Entropic forces

Why does a stretched rubber band pull back, and why does it get warm?

Rubber is made of long, floppy molecules. Each chain here is a string of beads held between a fixed post on the left and a handle on a spring on the right, jostled by heat. Nothing in the chain wants to be short, yet it drags the handle in and stretches the spring. Heat it and see what happens to the pull.

These chains are insulated: no heat bath, so their energy only changes by the work done on them. Stretch them quickly and the beads jiggle faster (they turn red): the temperature rises. Release them quickly and it falls.

Feeling it

In part 1 each chain's right end is a handle on a soft spring to a fixed post. The chain pulls the handle in until the spring pulls back as hard, so the stretched spring (blue when slack, red when taut) shows the chain's pull, and its length measures it. Raise the temperature and the springs stretch further: a hotter rubber band pulls harder. Heat a rubber band holding a weight and it lifts the weight.

The force is entropy

A floppy chain can wiggle into an enormous number of shapes. Most of them are crumpled; only a few are nearly straight. Holding the ends apart rules out the crumpled shapes, and the chain's constant thermal jiggling keeps tugging the ends toward the arrangements it can explore more freely. The bonds themselves do not get much longer: the pull comes from the number of shapes, that is, from entropy.

The test is temperature, a theme on every page of this site: effects driven by entropy get stronger as it gets hotter. An ordinary spring's pull comes from stretched bonds and hardly depends on temperature. An entropic spring's pull is the temperature times how fast the number of shapes falls as you stretch, f = −T ∂S/∂x, so it grows in proportion to T. Record the pull at a few temperatures: the points fall on a line through the origin. That is also why a rubber band holding a weight shortens when you heat it, the opposite of most materials.

Why stretching warms it

In part 2 there is no heat bath. Press Stretch fast and the right-hand ends are pulled away quickly. Straightening the chains removes shape entropy: fewer shapes are possible. In an insulated, quick stretch the total entropy cannot fall (nothing carries it away), so it has to reappear somewhere else, and the only place left is the beads' motion: they jiggle faster, which is to say the temperature rises. In energy terms, the pull you work against is what feeds them. Release fast runs it backwards: the chains crumple, gaining shape entropy, paid for by the beads' motion, and the temperature falls. Touch a rubber band to your lips, stretch it quickly, and you feel the warmth; let it snap back and it feels cool.

Caveats

  • Real rubber is a cross-linked network of chains in 3D; here each chain is separate and lives in 2D, where a chain cannot cross itself. The entropic argument is the same.
  • The beads are bonded by stiff springs (the Kremer–Grest model), so part of the pull is energetic: above T ≈ 1 the pull divided by T is constant to a few percent, but at T = 0.5 it is a third lower.
  • The surrounding solvent is not simulated; a heat bath acting on every bead stands in for it.
