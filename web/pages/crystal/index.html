What you are seeing

The atoms attract each other at a distance and repel up close (the Lennard-Jones force, a good model of argon). Cold, they settle into the arrangement that packs them best: the face-centred cubic crystal, each atom with twelve neighbours in the same pattern. The colour asks each atom whether its neighbours are arranged around it the way they are arranged around them (Steinhardt's bond-order test): in a crystal they all agree; in a liquid they do not.

Melting: where the heat goes

The heater adds heat in exactly counted amounts (it speeds every atom up by the same factor). At first the heat goes into speed, and the temperature rises. Then the crystal starts to melt, from its surface, where atoms have fewer neighbours holding them, and the temperature stalls: the heat now goes into pulling atoms out of the crystal's tight, low-energy arrangement into the looser liquid. That is the latent heat of melting. The temperature climbs again once the crystal is gone.

Freezing: why the liquid waits

Cool the drop below its melting point and it does not freeze at once. A crystal has to start somewhere, as a tiny cluster of ordered atoms, and a tiny cluster is mostly surface: its surface costs more than its ordered inside gains, so small clusters keep forming and dissolving. Only when one, by chance, grows past a critical size does growing become downhill, and then the whole drop freezes from it. The colder the liquid, the smaller that critical size and the sooner it happens. Run natively, a drop of 8,788 atoms held at kT = 0.42 stayed liquid for about 320 τ, then froze in the next 250 τ. Supercooled water does the same: clouds hold liquid droplets far below 0 °C, and a bottle of very pure water can stay liquid in a freezer until a knock sets it off.

The triple point

This crystal sits in an otherwise empty box, so as it warms, a few atoms evaporate from it: there is always a thin vapour around it. When it starts to melt, crystal, liquid and vapour are all there at once, and the temperature stalls at about kT = 0.62 until the crystal is gone. That stall is the triple point: the one temperature (and, with it, the one pressure, the vapour's) at which all three phases can sit side by side in balance.

Why only one? Two phases balance when their Gibbs free energies per molecule are equal. For liquid and vapour that is one condition on two knobs, temperature and pressure, so it holds along a line: the boiling point at each pressure. Solid and liquid balance along another line, the melting point at each pressure. Three phases need two conditions at once (solid = liquid and liquid = vapour), and two lines cross at a single point. Water's is at 0.01 °C and 0.006 atmospheres.

So is the ice in your drink at the triple point? No. The ice and the water are at 0 °C, on the melting line at the pressure they feel, one atmosphere, which is 160 times the triple point's. The air above them does hold water vapour, but the vapour is only a small part of the air: its own partial pressure is set by the humidity, and nothing makes it equal to the vapour pressure of ice or water at 0 °C. The drink is not in equilibrium with the room either: the ice melts, the water evaporates or condenses on the glass. An iceberg is the same: ice and seawater balance, near the freezing point of seawater, under the whole atmosphere. In a sealed box with only water in it, ice, water and vapour would all settle at the triple point, as this crystal does in its empty box.

Entropy

The liquid has more entropy than the crystal (its atoms can be arranged in far more ways), so the higher the temperature, the more that counts in G = H − TS, and above the melting point it wins. The heat you put in while it melts is what the liquid's extra entropy costs: L = T ΔS. (See phase transitions for the same argument for boiling.)

Caveats

  • A small crystal: a few thousand atoms, so a fair fraction sit on its surface, which melts first and at a lower temperature than the inside would.
  • The crystal sits in an empty periodic box (three times its volume). A little of it evaporates when it is hot.
  • Lennard-Jones atoms cut off at 2.5 σ. For argon, σ ≈ 0.34 nm, ε/k ≈ 120 K and τ ≈ 2 ps: the crystal is about 7 nm across.
