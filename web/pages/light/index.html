Einstein, 1905: light quanta (and Planck, 1900)

Why does a hot stove glow red and not blue? And is light made of grains?

Heat a box and it fills with light. Classical physics says the light should share the heat equally among all its ways of vibrating, and since there are endlessly more short-wavelength ways than long ones, a hot stove should blaze ultraviolet. It glows red. Planck fixed it in 1900 by letting light's energy come only in lumps hν; Einstein, in 1905, said the lumps are real: light is made of grains.

The ultraviolet catastrophe

In a box, light can vibrate in a set of standing waves, its modes, and the number of modes with frequencies near ν grows as ν²: there are far more ways to fit short waves than long ones. Classical physics gives every mode the same average energy, kT (equipartition), so the energy at frequency ν would grow as ν² without end: a hot stove would glow violet, then ultraviolet, with infinite total energy. That is the red line, and it is what the "classical" button gives.

Planck's lumps

Planck supposed (1900) that light of frequency ν exchanges energy with matter only in lumps hν. A mode whose lump is much bigger than kT is then almost never excited: the walls rarely have that much to spare, e−hν/kT. The high modes freeze out, as the cold diamond's vibrations do in the Einstein solid, and the spectrum rises, peaks and falls: Planck's law. Its peak moves with temperature (Wien's law, λpeak ≈ 2.9 mm·K / T): a stove at 1,000 K glows dull red at the very edge of the visible, the sun's surface at 5,800 K white.

Einstein's grains, and the photoelectric effect

Einstein went further in 1905: he showed that the entropy of dim high-frequency light depends on its volume exactly as a gas's does, as if it were made of independent particles of energy hν each, and drew the consequence. A grain of light hitting a metal gives all its energy to one electron, which escapes with hν − W if hν is more than the work W needed to free it, and not at all otherwise, however many grains arrive. So: below a threshold frequency no electrons, however bright; above it, electrons at once, however dim; brighter light means more electrons, not faster ones; and the fastest electrons' energy rises in a straight line with frequency, of slope h. Millikan, who set out to disprove it, confirmed every point by 1916. The wave theory predicts the opposite at every point: any colour works if bright enough, after a delay while the energy builds up, and brighter means faster.

Caveats

  • Part 1 is not molecules: a Monte Carlo of the box's light modes (frequencies up to the ultraviolet, with ν² as many modes per frequency as in a real box), each gaining or losing a quantum hν at a time with the Metropolis rule at the walls' temperature: a heat bath. The classical button lets each mode's energy change continuously instead.
  • The glow's colour is Planck's spectrum weighted by rough sensitivities of the eye's three kinds of cone.
  • Part 2 is a model: grains arriving at random at a rate set by the brightness, each freeing an electron with hν − W less a random share lost on the way out (so hν − W is the most); the wave theory's electrons absorb energy continuously.

About this simulation

  • Method: Monte Carlo of the light's modes: 80 frequency bins up to 1.2 × 10¹⁵ Hz with 300 sample modes each (standing for the ν² modes per frequency of a real box); each mode is offered a gain or loss of one quantum hν (or, when classical, any amount up to kT) and takes it by the Metropolis rule. The photoelectric part is a model: grains arriving at random, or a wave pouring energy steadily onto 120 surface electrons.
  • Ensemble: canonical: the box's walls are a heat bath at the temperature you set, which the modes trade energy with.
  • Size: 24,000 modes; one sweep (every mode offered a change once) per frame. Time step of the photoelectric part: one frame.
