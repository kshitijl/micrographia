The evidence for atoms: Perrin, 1908

How did counting grains at different heights in a drop of water weigh a molecule?

Grains far too big to see molecules in, but small enough to jiggle, sink under gravity. They do not all pile on the floor: the jiggling holds them up, and they settle into a thinning haze, like a tiny atmosphere. Jean Perrin counted such grains, height by height, under a microscope. From how fast their number falls with height he got the mass of a hydrogen atom.

The tiny atmosphere

Each grain is jostled by the molecules around it, and in equilibrium it carries the same average energy as they do, ½kT per direction of motion (heavy and light molecules): a grain is just a very big molecule. So the grains obey the same law as the air: their number thins with height as e−mgh/kT (the atmosphere). The height over which it thins by a factor e is H = kT/(mg). The air's H is about 8 km; for grains a billion billion times heavier than an air molecule it is a fraction of a millimetre, small enough to see under a microscope.

Weighing a molecule

Perrin knew the grains' mass m (from their size and density, measured separately) and g, and he measured H by counting. That gives kT = mgH. And he knew R, the gas constant, from the gas laws, per mole of gas: R = N k. So N = RT/(mgH): Avogadro's number, from counting grains. Perrin found 6.8 × 10²³ (the modern value is 6.02 × 10²³), from grains of gamboge resin about 0.4 µm across.

Here the same arithmetic is done in Perrin's units: the grains are given his gamboge's mass, and the box is scaled so that its grains' haze is as tall as his. The readout is what he would have got from this count. (The simulation knows its own N; the point is that the count recovers it.)

Entropy against gravity

Gravity alone would put every grain on the floor; the grains' entropy (all the ways they can be spread through the height) holds them up. The balance is the haze. Warm the gas and the entropy wins more: the haze rises, H growing in proportion to T.

Caveats

  • 2D, and the liquid is a thin gas of small molecules, so buoyancy (which Perrin, in water, had to subtract: he used m minus the mass of water displaced) is only about 1% here and is ignored.
  • The grains are only three molecules across; near the floor they crowd each other, so the fit uses the heights above the bottom 6 σ.
  • No heat bath: the energy is conserved (gravity's potential energy included); warming or cooling adds or removes a counted amount.
