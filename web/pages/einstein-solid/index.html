Einstein, 1907: the quantum solid

Why does a cold diamond need almost no heat to warm it? And how does energy spread itself out?

Two blocks of a solid, each a grid of atoms vibrating in place. In Einstein's picture each atom's vibration holds energy only in whole lumps, quanta of size ε. The quanta hop at random from atom to atom, every arrangement as likely as any other. That is all; watch what comes out.

Energy spreads because there are more ways for it to be spread

Nothing pushes the quanta anywhere: each hop goes to a random atom. Yet heat flows from the full block to the empty one, and the atoms' quanta settle into Boltzmann's distribution, many atoms with none, fewer with one, fewer still with two, each step down by the same factor e−ε/kT. Both happen because those are the arrangements there are most of. The number of ways to share q quanta among N atoms is Ω = (q + N − 1)! / (q! (N − 1)!); S = k ln Ω is the entropy (the readout). Spreading energy across both blocks has astronomically more ways than keeping it in one, so that is what you see. The temperature is how fast the entropy rises as energy is added, 1/T = dS/dE: the block whose entropy rises faster per quantum is the colder one, and it takes quanta until the rates are equal.

Why the cold solid hardly needs heat

Classically every atom holds kT of vibrational energy per direction, whatever T: the heat capacity is a constant, 3k per atom in 3D (the Dulong–Petit law, which most solids obey at room temperature). But when kT is much smaller than a quantum ε, almost every atom holds none, and adding a little heat excites hardly any: the heat capacity falls towards zero, as e−ε/kT. Einstein's 1907 paper explained in this way why diamond, whose stiff bonds make its quanta big, has a heat capacity far below Dulong and Petit's at room temperature and almost none when cold: one of the first predictions of quantum theory outside light.

Caveats

  • Not molecular dynamics: a Monte Carlo of quanta, the counting behind Einstein's model. Each step picks two atoms at random and moves a quantum from the first to the second, if it has one: this visits every arrangement equally often, as the second law assumes.
  • Einstein's model gives every atom the same frequency; real solids have a spread (Debye's model), which makes the heat capacity fall as T³ rather than exponentially at the lowest temperatures.
  • One direction of vibration per atom (a 1D oscillator), so the classical value is 1k per atom.

About this simulation

  • Method: Monte Carlo of quanta: two blocks of 30 × 30 Einstein oscillators, each holding a whole number of quanta ε; a step moves one quantum from a random atom to another (across the contact when the blocks touch).
  • Ensemble: microcanonical: the total number of quanta, so the energy, is fixed except when you add heat; every arrangement of the quanta is equally likely in the long run.
  • Size: 900 atoms a block; 40,000 hops per frame.
