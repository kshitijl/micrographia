Equipartition

At the same temperature, does a heavy molecule have less energy than a light one?

Here light molecules, molecules sixteen times heavier, and a few “bowling balls”, each as wide as six molecules and hundreds of times heavier, all share one box. They trade energy with every collision. Which ones end up with more?

Reach in

Drag a box on the simulation to select the molecules in it, then kick them (three times faster, nine times the energy), freeze them, or shove them all to the right. The total energy jumps up or down at once, and then stays put: nothing else adds or removes energy. Watch the averages too: the kicked molecules share their extra energy with everyone, and every kind, light, heavy and ball, ends up with the same average again.

The entropy plot shows how spread out the molecules' energies are (Boltzmann's H-function), against the most it could be for the current total energy. A kick or a freeze makes the energies lopsided, opening a gap below the ceiling; collisions close it again within a few τ. The trails behind the balls are their recent paths: Brownian motion.

Same energy, different speeds

The top chart is each kind's average kinetic energy, with the line at kT. All three settle to the same value: on average every molecule carries ½kT of kinetic energy for each direction it can move, whatever its mass. This is equipartition. Collisions keep passing energy around until no kind has more than its share, because a fast heavy ball hitting a slow light molecule tends to give energy away, and the reverse tends to take it.

Since energy is ½mv², the same energy means a heavier molecule moves more slowly: the typical speed goes as 1/√m (lower chart). The balls amble around at a fraction of the light molecules' speed, kicked this way and that by thousands of tiny collisions.

Brownian motion

That jittery wandering of the balls is Brownian motion, first seen in pollen grains in water in 1827. In 1905 Einstein showed that it is exactly this: big particles with a share kT of energy like everything else, buffeted by unseen molecules. From how fast the grains wander, Jean Perrin then measured the size of molecules and Avogadro's number. The trails show the balls' recent paths; make a ball heavier and it slows down, but its average energy stays the same.

Caveats

  • 2D, soft repulsive molecules: no attraction, no rotation or vibration (real molecules store energy in those too, ½kT per quadratic term).
  • With only a few balls their average converges slowly; restart the averages after changing the mass.
