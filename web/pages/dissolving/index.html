Why does salt dissolve in water? And why does more dissolve when it is hot?

A crystal of salt, its two kinds of ion (red and gold) holding each other tightly, sits in water (blue). The ions are attracted to the water too, but less than to each other: dissolving costs energy. Yet one by one they leave the crystal and wander off, more of them the hotter the water. What pays?

Entropy pays

In the crystal each ion sits among opposite ions it attracts strongly; in the water it sits among water molecules it attracts less. So dissolving costs energy (here, as for ordinary salt, the water cools a little as salt dissolves: ΔH > 0). What it gains is entropy: an ion in the crystal has one place, an ion in solution can be anywhere in the water. Free energy G = H − TS decides, and the balance stops when the solution is so full that one more dissolved ion gains too little entropy to pay: saturation. The concentration there is the solubility.

Why hotter dissolves more

The entropy gain is multiplied by T in G = H − TS, so the hotter it is, the more it counts against the fixed energy cost, and the fuller the solution can get before it stops: solubility rises with temperature. (This is van 't Hoff's rule: when dissolving takes in heat, warming pushes it further, as Le Chatelier said.) Measured natively, after 2,000 τ: about 63 of the 256 ions dissolved at kT 0.7, 171 at 1.1. A salt whose dissolving gives out heat would do the opposite, and some do (cerium sulphate; and gases: why warm soda goes flat).

Why salt changes the melting point so much more than the boiling point

See salt and ice: it is the same mixing entropy, set against the latent heat, and water's latent heat of boiling is almost seven times its latent heat of melting.

Does salt in the water change how much sugar dissolves?

A little. Dissolved ions hold water molecules around them, and those water molecules are less free to surround anything else, so a salty solution dissolves somewhat less of a neutral molecule like sugar (or a gas): "salting out". But each solute's entropy of mixing is its own, so to a first approximation they dissolve independently.

Caveats

  • 2D, and the ions are not charged: opposite ions attract by a strong short-range Lennard-Jones force (ε = 3), like ions only repel, and water attracts both (ε = 1.3). Real ions attract water through its dipole, and each other over long range.
  • The box is full of water (no vapour), dense enough to stay liquid over this whole temperature range; a heat bath holds the temperature.
  • "Dissolved" means no opposite ion within 1.4 σ: ions in pairs count with the crystal.
