The evidence for atoms: specific heats (Dulong and Petit, 1819; Maxwell's "greatest difficulty")

Why does it take more heat to warm some gases than others? And why don't argon atoms spin?

Give the same heat to a gramme-molecule of argon and one of nitrogen, and the argon gets hotter. The heat goes into the molecules' motions, shared equally among every way they can move: argon's atoms can only fly about, but a nitrogen molecule can also tumble and vibrate, so its heat is spread thinner. Count the ways and you predict the heat per degree. That worked, mostly, and where it failed it was the first sign of quantum mechanics.

Slide the temperature down. Below about 100 K hydrogen stops spinning: its heat per degree drops to that of a single atom, 1.5k. Its vibration is frozen at all ordinary temperatures, nitrogen's too, so at room temperature both take 2.5k, not the 3.5k the classical count gives. Argon never spins at all.

Counting the ways to move

Equipartition says that, in equilibrium, every independent way a molecule can hold energy that goes as a square (a velocity in one direction, a spin about one axis, the stretch of a spring) holds ½kT on average. In 2D an atom has two directions to fly in: 1k of heat per degree. A two-atom molecule adds a spin (½k) and a vibration, which holds energy twice over, moving and stretched (1k): 2.5k. An atom in a crystal cannot fly, but vibrates in two directions, each moving and stretched: 2k (in 3D, 3k: Dulong and Petit's law, 1819, the same for every element's atoms, which let chemists check atomic weights). Measured natively: 1.01, 2.58 and 1.98.

The greatest difficulty

Real gases do not do quite this. Nitrogen at room temperature takes the heat per degree of translation plus rotation and no vibration, as if its spring were frozen solid; and hydrogen, cooled below about 100 K, stops rotating too. Maxwell called it the greatest difficulty the kinetic theory had met; classical mechanics cannot freeze a motion, as part 1 shows (a stiffer spring makes no difference: 2.39 at stiffness 1,000 against 2.58 at 200, the same within the noise).

The answer is quanta, as in the Einstein solid: a spin or a vibration can only take energy in steps, and when the first step is much bigger than kT the collisions almost never supply it. The motion is frozen out. Hydrogen's spin steps are big because the molecule is light and small (θrot ≈ 85 K); nitrogen's are tiny (3 K), but both vibrate stiffly (6,300 K and 3,400 K). And an argon atom? Its mass sits in the nucleus, 10,000 times smaller than the atom, so its moment of inertia is tiny and its first step of spin enormous (around 10⁹ K, Perrin's §94): it never spins, which is why a monatomic gas has only its translations, as Perrin argued from the specific heats to the nucleus.
