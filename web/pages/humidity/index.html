Saturation is a balance, not a container

Air does not "hold" water like a sponge: the air molecules here do nothing for the water, and the same amount of vapour would sit over the pool in a vacuum. Molecules leave the liquid's surface when one happens to get enough energy to break free of its neighbours' attraction, and vapour molecules hitting the surface stick. When the two rates are equal the vapour is saturated: that is its equilibrium, the liquid's vapour pressure. Over a pool it settles there by itself; above it, the excess condenses back; below it, more evaporates.

Why warmer means more

Escaping takes an energy L per molecule (the latent heat), and the share of molecules with that much to spare goes as e−L/kT: steeply up with temperature. So the saturated vapour roughly doubles with every few degrees: here from 0.023 per σ² at kT 0.40 to 0.058 at 0.44 (measured by the Gibbs-ensemble method on liquid meets vapour, and held steady by this simulation). For water, saturated air holds about twice as much for every 10 °C warmer: 4.8 g per m³ at 0 °C, 17 at 20 °C, 30 at 30 °C (the Clausius–Clapeyron equation).

Dew, fog and clouds

Cool humid air, and the vapour it carries can be more than saturation at the new temperature: the excess condenses, onto whatever is there (the pool; a cold glass; dust in the air: clouds; grass at night: dew). Over tiny droplets, though, the vapour stays a little above saturation: a molecule on a sharply curved surface has fewer neighbours holding it, and escapes more easily (the Kelvin effect), so fog lingers until its droplets merge or fall, and clouds need dust to start on. The dew point is the temperature at which the vapour now in the air would be saturated: anything colder than that gets wet. Breathing on a cold window raises the vapour near it above saturation at the window's temperature, and it fogs.

Caveats

  • A 2D Lennard-Jones liquid, not water, with its narrow liquid range (kT ≈ 0.38–0.46 here).
  • Evaporation in 2D is slow: a vapour space started empty takes over a thousand τ to fill. So the air starts saturated, and condensation (which is fast) is easier to watch than evaporation.
  • A gentle heat bath holds the temperature (the experiment is about temperature); the cold glass is a heat-bath wall at a lower temperature.
  • The vapour density counts the water molecules more than 8 σ above the pool and below the ceiling that are single or in clusters of fewer than 8 (a saturated vapour has a few dimers and trimers); bigger clusters are droplets, counted as fog.
