Why does the boiling point rise with pressure? (And why does a pressure cooker cook faster?)

A droplet of 500 molecules in a sealed box with no gravity, heated in exactly counted amounts (the heater, or bursts). Watch where the heat goes: into speed (the temperature) or into pulling molecules out of the liquid against their attraction (the latent heat). While the droplet evaporates, more than half of every bit of heat goes into pulling apart, so the temperature climbs slowly; once it is all vapour, every bit goes into speed.
Evaporation is slow here: a molecule leaves the surface only now and then, and the vapour in 2D is very thin. Heat faster than it can evaporate and the liquid overheats, climbing past its critical point (kT ≈ 0.46, above which there is no liquid at all) before the droplet is gone. For the flattest curve, set the heater to its lowest setting and the speed high, and give it several minutes. In a sealed box the boiling point also rises as the vapour builds up (as in a pressure cooker), so even then the shelf tilts; in an open pan, at constant pressure, it is flat.

Escaping and returning

A molecule at the surface of a liquid is held by the attraction of its neighbours. To escape it needs an extra energy, roughly the latent heat per molecule L, and the fraction of molecules with that much to spare is the Boltzmann factor e−L/kT. Meanwhile vapour molecules that hit the surface stick. Escape and return balance when the vapour reaches a certain density, and so a certain pressure, the vapour pressure:

pvap ∝ e−L/kT

Plot ln pvap against 1/T and you get a straight line whose slope is −L/k (this is the Clausius–Clapeyron relation). Record a few temperatures and the page fits the line and reports L. Because of the exponential, a small rise in temperature raises the vapour pressure a lot.

Boiling is when the vapour pressure reaches the outside pressure

Below the boiling point, evaporation happens only at the surface. Boiling starts when the vapour pressure equals the pressure of the surroundings: then bubbles of vapour can form inside the liquid without being crushed. Raise the surrounding pressure and the liquid must get hotter before its vapour pressure catches up. A pressure cooker roughly doubles the pressure, which for water moves the boiling point from 100 °C to about 120 °C, and chemical reactions in food run several times faster at 120 °C. In Leh, at 3,500 m, the air pressure is about two thirds of sea level and water boils near 88 °C, which is why dal takes so long to cook there.

Why the temperature stops rising while it boils

Once a liquid boils at a given pressure, extra heat goes into pulling molecules out of the liquid (the latent heat) rather than into making them faster, so the temperature stays put until the liquid is gone.

Caveats

  • A 2D Lennard-Jones liquid: its liquid range is narrow (it freezes a little below 0.4 and its critical point is a little above 0.46 in these units), so the temperature range here is small, and the vapour is very dilute.
  • The vapour pressure is taken as vapour density × kT (the vapour is nearly ideal): counting the vapour molecules is far less noisy than timing their rare hits on the ceiling.
  • A weak gravity holds the liquid at the bottom; a heat bath keeps the temperature, or (choose the hot plate) the floor heats the liquid from below through an insulating box, which heats it until everything reaches the plate's temperature. Record points only once the measured temperature has caught up.
