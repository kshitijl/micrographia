Energy against entropy

Why don't oil and water mix?

Oil and water molecules attract each other: weakly, but they do. And mixing raises entropy, which nature favours. So why do they separate? Here are two kinds of molecules that attract everything, their own kind a little more. Tune the attraction between the two kinds, and the temperature, and see who wins.

Salad dressing

Whisk oil and vinegar together and you get a cloudy mixture of tiny droplets. That is not oil and vinegar mixing; it is you, pushing the liquid far from equilibrium, breaking the oil into droplets faster than they can find each other. Stop whisking and the droplets drift, touch and merge, and in a few minutes the dressing has separated again, without gravity doing anything special. Stir the simulation with the spoon and watch the same thing: the stirring tears the domains apart, and as soon as you stop they coarsen back. (Stirring also warms the liquid a little, as whisking does; the heat bath takes it away again.) Mustard or egg yolk in a dressing are emulsifiers: molecules that sit at the oil–water boundary and slow the droplets' merging, so the mixture lasts longer.

With and without gravity

With gravity off, oil and water still separate, into blobs and stripes wherever they happen to form: the separation is the attraction and the entropy, not weight. Turn gravity on and the lighter oil domains float up (their combined buoyancy is much stronger than any single molecule's), so the oil collects on top, as in a bottle on the shelf.

The tug of war

Every molecule here attracts every other one. But a water molecule surrounded by water feels more attraction (and so has lower energy) than one surrounded by oil, because the oil–water attraction is weaker. Separating into regions maximises like–like contacts and lowers the total energy. Mixing, on the other hand, has more arrangements: entropy pulls towards mixing.

Which wins depends on temperature, because what nature minimises at fixed temperature is the free energy F = E − TS. At low temperature the energy term dominates and the kinds separate; heat them up and the entropy term takes over and they mix. Set the oil–water attraction equal to the like–like attraction (1.0) and there is no energy reason to separate at all: they mix at any temperature. Try making it larger than 1, and like prefers unlike.

Real oil and water are stranger

This model captures the classic “regular solution” picture used for many liquid mixtures, and some real pairs (like methanol and hexane) do separate cold and mix hot. Oil and water are more subtle: water molecules near oil arrange their hydrogen bonds into ordered cages, so for oil in water mixing actually lowers entropy at room temperature. This “hydrophobic effect” needs a model of water's hydrogen bonds, and will get its own page.

Caveats

  • 2D Lennard-Jones molecules of equal size and mass; a heat bath acting throughout the volume keeps the temperature fixed.
  • The “mixed” measure looks at small regions of about 16 molecules: 100% when the share of oil varies between regions only as much as chance allows, 0% when every region holds only one kind.
