The Boltzmann distribution

Where does the Boltzmann distribution come from?

The textbook picture: many boxes of gas side by side. Molecules bounce around inside their own box and can never leave it, but they push on their neighbours through the walls, so energy leaks from box to box. The whole set is sealed off, so its total energy never changes. Start the molecules with energies that look nothing like Boltzmann's rule, and watch every box find it.

What you're seeing

Molecules are coloured by speed. Every start gives the whole set the same total energy, an average of exactly 1 kT per molecule, and gives each molecule in a group exactly the same energy, so the starting histogram is a few spikes. Within a few τ the collisions inside each box smear the spikes into a smooth curve; over the next hundred τ the walls even out the boxes. The total energy line stays flat the whole time, while the outlined box's temperature wanders, settles and then keeps fluctuating a little.

How energy gets through a wall that molecules can't

The forces are computed the same way everywhere: every pair of molecules closer than the repulsion's range (1.12 σ; the molecules repel and do not attract) pushes on each other, whichever boxes they are in. The walls between boxes affect only motion: a molecule reaching one has the component of its velocity across the wall reversed, an exact bounce that keeps its energy. So a molecule just left of a wall and one just right of it push each other apart exactly as if the wall were not there. When a fast molecule comes up to the wall it shoves a slow one on the other side away, slowing down itself: energy passes across, molecules stay put. It is how heat goes through the wall of a mug, which is also made of atoms jostling their neighbours. Exactly the molecules within 1.12 σ of a wall (and of a molecule across it) take part, which is why the boxes even out much more slowly than each box settles inside.

Reach in

Drag a box on the simulation to select molecules, then kick them, freeze them or shove them. The total energy jumps and then stays flat. The kicked molecules' energies make the histogram lopsided, the entropy drops below its ceiling, and within a few τ collisions spread the energy and the entropy climbs back.

Where the exponential comes from

Think of one molecule, or one box, and everything else as its surroundings. If our molecule has energy E, the rest must share Etotal − E, and the chance of that is proportional to the number of ways they can do it. Taking a slice of energy from a huge system shrinks its number of arrangements by the same factor each time you take another slice: that factor is e−1/kT per unit of energy, which is what temperature means. So the chance of energy E goes as e−E/kT. Nothing tells the molecules to follow this rule; it is only counting, done by the collisions. The blue curve is that rule at the final temperature; the boxes reach it from any start.

P(E) ∝ Ωrest(Etotal − E) ∝ e−E/kT

Entropy goes up

The entropy plotted is Boltzmann's H-function: −Σ p ln p over the histogram of molecular energies, in units of k per molecule. It measures how spread out the energies are. Spikes have low entropy; of all ways to spread a fixed average energy, the exponential is the one with the most, so the curve climbs to its ceiling (1 + ln kT, the grey line) and stays there. That is the Boltzmann distribution derived the other way round: it is the most probable way to share the energy. Boltzmann proved in 1872 that collisions make H increase.

Boxes fluctuate too

Even at the end each box's temperature jitters by about 12%. A box of 64 molecules in contact with the others is a small system in a heat bath, and its energy fluctuates by about 1/√64 of its mean. For a glass of water, with 1025 molecules, the same rule gives fluctuations of one part in 1012, which is why everyday objects seem to have one definite temperature.

So why does Maxwell's speed distribution peak away from zero?

Switch the view to speeds. The most likely energy is zero, yet the most likely speed is not. In two dimensions a velocity can point anywhere on a circle of radius v, and that circle grows with v. Multiply the shrinking Boltzmann factor by the growing circle and you get a peak at v = √(kT/m). In 2D the growth exactly cancels the change of variables, so the energy distribution is a pure exponential with no peak at all. (In 3D the energy distribution itself has a peak, because the sphere of velocities grows faster.)

Caveats

  • The walls between boxes stop molecules but pass on their pushes: a molecule near a wall is repelled by molecules just across it. That is the only coupling; there is no thermostat.
  • Total energy includes the molecules' small potential energy. It is constant to a few tenths of a percent over hundreds of τ; the remainder is the integrator's step-by-step error.
  • The entropy counts only how energies are spread, not positions, and is estimated from a histogram (with the usual small-sample correction), so it sits a little below its ceiling.
