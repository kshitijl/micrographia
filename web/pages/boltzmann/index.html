The Boltzmann distribution

Why is one molecule's energy distributed exponentially when the whole gas has a fixed total?

This gas is sealed off from everything: its total energy never changes. Yet any single molecule's energy jumps around wildly as it collides, and over time it follows a precise rule: the chance of having energy E falls off as e−E/kT. Watch that rule appear, then watch it melt into a narrow bell curve as you track bigger groups of molecules.

Where the exponential comes from

Follow one molecule. The other 19,999 hold the rest of the energy, and they are vastly more numerous. If our molecule has energy E, the others must share Etotal − E, and the number of ways they can do that is what decides how likely it is. Taking a little energy from a huge system shrinks its number of arrangements by the same factor each time you take another slice: that factor is e−1/kT per unit of energy, which is in fact what temperature means. So the chance of energy E goes as e−E/kT. Nothing told the molecule to follow this rule; it is just counting, done by the collisions.

P(E) ∝ Ωrest(Etotal − E) ∝ e−E/kT

Bigger groups: from Boltzmann to thermodynamics

Now track groups of molecules. A group's energy is a sum of many nearly independent pieces, so it piles up near the average and its spread shrinks like 1/√(group size): for 1,000 molecules it is about 3% of the mean. For the 1023 molecules in a glass of water the spread is about one part in 1011, which is why everyday objects seem to have one definite energy and temperature. The theory curve is the gamma distribution, the exact result for a sum of exponential pieces.

So why does Maxwell's speed distribution peak away from zero?

Switch the view to speeds. The most likely energy is zero, yet the most likely speed is not. The difference is how many ways there are to have a given speed: in two dimensions a velocity can point anywhere on a circle of radius v, and that circle grows with v. Multiply the shrinking Boltzmann factor by the growing circle and you get a peak at v = √(kT/m). In 2D that growth exactly cancels the change of variables, so the energy distribution is a pure exponential with no peak at all. (In 3D the energy distribution itself has a peak, because the sphere of velocities grows faster.)

Caveats

  • Kinetic energies only; the molecules also have potential energy from their neighbours, which is part of the fixed total. The kinetic part follows the Boltzmann rule exactly in classical physics, whatever the forces.
  • Groups are fixed sets of molecules scattered through the box, not a region of space.
  • Velocities are the integrator's half-step values, accurate to about 0.01%.
