Phase transitions, three ways

Why do liquid and vapour sit side by side, and why does the difference vanish at the critical point?

Below its critical temperature a substance splits into a dense liquid and a thin vapour, at two definite densities; heat it and the two densities creep together until, at the critical point, they merge and the boundary between them dissolves into flickering clusters of every size. Molecular dynamics is a slow way to see this: here are three faster ones, each simplifying something different.

What the lattice gas keeps, and what it throws away

It keeps the two things that make a liquid–gas transition: molecules attract their neighbours (energy, favouring the clumped liquid) and they wander (entropy, favouring the spread-out vapour). It throws away velocities, momentum and the continuous positions of molecular dynamics. Each step picks a neighbouring molecule and hole and lets the molecule hop with the Metropolis probability min(1, e−ΔE/kT): downhill hops always, uphill ones with the Boltzmann factor. Molecules are never created or destroyed. This makes it cheap: a million sites, a thousand sweeps a second on a laptop's GPU.

It is the Ising model of magnetism in disguise (a molecule is a spin up, a hole a spin down), which Onsager solved exactly in 1944. So the coexisting densities here can be checked against an exact formula, (1 ± M)/2 with M = (1 − sinh(ε/2kT)−4)1/8, and they agree (measured natively: vapour 0.0450 against 0.0443 at kT = 0.5, liquid 0.957 against 0.956).

The critical point

As the temperature rises the vapour fills up and the liquid thins, and at kTc = 0.567 they are the same: there is just one fluid. Near it, regions of liquid and vapour of every size come and go, from single sites to the whole box; in a real fluid this makes it milky (critical opalescence), because clusters the size of the wavelength of light scatter it. Remarkably, the way the densities merge (M ∝ (Tc − T)1/8 in 2D) is the same for every fluid and every magnet in two dimensions, whatever the molecules: near a critical point only dimension and symmetry matter (universality).

Entropy

The vapour has more entropy per molecule (more places to be) and the liquid less energy (more bonds). Which wins depends on temperature, through the free energy F = E − TS: the hotter it is, the more the entropy counts, so the more molecules the vapour holds at coexistence.

Lattice Boltzmann: what it keeps

Lattice Boltzmann keeps what the lattice gas throws away: momentum and flow. Each site carries the amounts of fluid moving in nine directions (still, the four neighbours, the four diagonals). Each step they all move one site and then relax towards the mix that a fluid at rest, or moving, would have there. Averaged, that is exactly the Navier–Stokes equations of a viscous fluid. To make it separate into liquid and vapour, Shan and Chen added an attraction between neighbouring sites; here its strength is set from a real equation of state (Carnahan–Starling, a better van der Waals), so the fluid has a temperature and a critical point. It then makes bubbles, drops, surface tension (a small drop is rounder and has a higher pressure inside, as Laplace said) and coalescence by itself, at a few hundred million site updates a second.

With the hot plate on, a temperature field rides along with the fluid: heat diffuses from the plate, flows with the fluid, and is taken up where liquid expands into vapour, the latent heat. Above the plate the liquid becomes superheated, bubbles nucleate and grow, and gravity lifts them away: nucleate boiling. Measured natively, its liquid densities match what the Shan–Chen model predicts for this equation of state to 0.2% (0.3325 against 0.3321 at 0.75 Tc); its vapour is less accurate (0.0109 against 0.0086; 0.0291 against 0.0278 at 0.85 Tc).

Caveats

  • Lattice Boltzmann's units are the lattice's: the liquid here is only ten to thirty times denser than its vapour (water's liquid is 1,600 times denser than its steam), interfaces are a few sites thick, and small spurious currents circulate around drops.
  • Lattice gas, no momentum: molecules diffuse rather than flow, so droplets grow only by molecules wandering from one to another (and liquid falls under gravity only by hopping down). The next method, lattice Boltzmann, has flow.
  • The hot plate holds the bottom rows at a different temperature, a local heat bath: the moves there use its temperature.
  • The densities above are read from 6 × 6 blocks: blocks fuller than ¾ count as liquid, emptier than ¼ as vapour. Near the critical point there are no such blocks to speak of.
