The second law, up close

Can heat ever flow from cold to hot?

The second law says heat flows from hot to cold. That is a statement about averages. Here are 128 tiny systems, each a dozen molecules between a hot wall (below) and a cold wall (above). We measure how much heat each one passes upward in a short time window, and count how often it goes the wrong way.

How the walls move heat

The walls are heat baths. Whatever speed a molecule hits a wall with, it leaves with a fresh random velocity, drawn from the distribution of molecules crossing a surface in a gas at the wall's temperature (panel 2). So each hit is a small exchange of energy: the wall gains whatever the molecule brought in minus what it leaves with. On average a hot wall sends molecules off faster than they came and a cold wall slower, so energy flows from the hot wall, through the molecules' collisions, to the cold one. That is heat conduction.

What you're seeing

The molecules are coloured by speed, blue to red. Molecules that hit the bottom wall leave with the hot wall's temperature; those that hit the top wall leave with the cold wall's. Each column is its own little system. Every time window, the page reads how much energy each column took from the hot wall and gave to the cold wall, and averages the two to get the heat Q it passed from hot to cold.

Backwards, but rarely, and predictably

Most windows pass heat the normal way, but a sizeable fraction pass it backwards. A wall gains heat when a molecule arrives faster than the wall sends it back, and loses heat when it sends a molecule back faster than it arrived. Backwards means the opposite of the usual: molecules that happened to arrive at the hot wall faster than it returned them (heating it further), and molecules the cold wall happened to send back faster than they came (cooling it further). The second law does not forbid this; it says such windows become rare as the system or the time gets bigger. The fluctuation theorem makes that exact. The odds of passing heat Q backwards rather than forwards are

P(−Q) / P(Q) = e−Q (1/kTc − 1/kTh)

so the lower chart, ln[P(Q)/P(−Q)] against Q, should be a straight line with slope 1/Tc − 1/Th. Note that the right-hand side is just e−ΔS/k, where ΔS is the entropy the forward heat flow would have produced. A cup of tea cooling by one joule would have to fluctuate backwards against odds of about e−1020. Small systems, like the molecular machines in your cells, live in the regime where these backward steps are common.

Caveats

  • The theorem is exact in the limit of long windows. Over short windows each column's own energy changes too, which bends the line slightly; averaging the heat taken from the hot wall and given to the cold wall reduces that.
  • Windows of the same column are correlated in time, so the error bars are underestimates.
  • Small-system violations of the second law were observed in the lab with a bead in an optical trap (Wang et al., 2002) [from memory; verify the reference].
