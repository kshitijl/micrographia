Three ways to hit a fibre

A sieve stops what is bigger than its holes. A filter catches particles by bringing them into contact with a fibre, where they stick (van der Waals forces hold them). Air flows round each fibre, and a particle that followed the air exactly would flow round it too. Particles do not:

  • Diffusion. Small particles are knocked about by air molecules and wander sideways off the airflow (Brownian motion, the same jostling as on the smell page). The slower the flow, the more time they have to wander into a fibre.
  • Inertia. Heavy particles cannot turn as sharply as the air does and run straight into the fibre in front of them.
  • Interception. A large particle following the air past a fibre touches it simply because it is large.

Diffusion works best on the smallest particles, inertia and interception on the largest, so a filter has a most penetrating size in between. For real fibre filters it is about 0.1–0.3 µm, and the N95 rating means at least 95% of such particles are caught: everything else is caught better. Respiratory droplets and aerosols are mostly larger. Many N95 fibres also carry electric charge, which pulls particles in from further away, a fourth mechanism not modelled here.

In this toy the fibres are 4 σ across with gaps of 8 σ, the air is a gas of molecules 1 σ across, and the aerosols range from air-molecule size to just smaller than the gaps. Even particles 27× smaller than the gaps are caught on a good fraction of passes, and the largest almost always. The toy does not resolve the most penetrating size: between the smallest and the middle sizes the difference is within the noise (it changes sign from run to run), because even the smallest particles here are as heavy as an air molecule and the air is too thin and fast for diffusion to dominate. Each extra row of fibres catches a share of what is left, which is why real masks are many fibres thick. As particles stick, the filter clogs and the same fan pushes less air through: a used mask is harder to breathe through.
