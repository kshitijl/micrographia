Protein folding, and what heat, acid and salt do to it

How does a protein fold itself up? And why do heat, vinegar or salt undo it?

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds, by itself, into one compact shape. Some of its links are oily (hydrophobic): water pushes them together into a core. Others carry charges, which pair up on the outside. Heat it, and the jiggling wins: the chain opens out (an egg white turns opaque as its proteins unfold and tangle). Acid or alkali charge up the outside until it repels itself apart; salt screens the charges.

Run: the stretched chain folds up, its oily links packing into a core. Raise the temperature slowly and watch the contacts melt away; the melting curve fills in. Then, back at body temperature, slide the pH to vinegar (3) or to soap (12): the chain swells. Add salt and it comes back together.

Folded by water, undone by entropy

An oily link in contact with water forces the water molecules around it into a cage, which costs them entropy; tucked against another oily link, it frees some of that water. So oily links attract, in effect (here, each touching pair gains 1 ε), and a chain with the right pattern of them has one compact shape with its oil inside. But there is only a handful of compact shapes, against billions of open ones. The free energy of the folded state is lower in energy (the contacts) but also in entropy (it is one shape); at a temperature where TΔS overtakes ΔH, the open coil wins. The melting curve shows where. Notice how the coil gains as the temperature rises: the story of this whole site, entropy mattering more when hotter.

Acid, alkali and salt

Its acidic links carry a negative charge unless the water is acid enough to put a proton back on them (below their pKa, about 4); the basic links carry a positive one unless the water is alkaline enough to take theirs off (above about 10.5). At neutral pH both are charged and pair up, + with −, helping to hold the shape. In vinegar only the positive charges are left, and they push each other apart: the chain swells (which is how acid "cooks" fish in ceviche). Salt's ions crowd around every charge and screen it (the Debye length, the reach of a charge, shrinks): the repulsion fades and the oil can pull the chain together again. That is also why proteins are stored in buffered, slightly salty solutions, and why a fever's few degrees are dangerous only for the proteins already close to their melting point.

Caveats

  • The HP lattice model (Lau and Dill, 1989), with charges: each link is one site on a square grid; real proteins are 3D chains of 20 kinds of amino acid, and their folding is far more cooperative (sharper melting) than this short chain's.
  • Water is implicit: the oily attraction stands for its entropy, and is held fixed here, though really it grows with temperature; the charges are mean charges at the pH (Henderson–Hasselbalch), with a screened (Debye–Hückel) interaction.
  • Cooked egg white does not refold on cooling, because its unfolded proteins tangle and stick to each other; a single chain alone, like this one, can refold.

About this simulation

  • Method: Monte Carlo (Metropolis) of a 24-link lattice chain in 2D (JavaScript): end moves, corner flips, crankshafts and pivots, each accepted with probability min(1, e−ΔE/kT).
  • Ensemble: canonical: the water is a heat bath at the temperature you set.
  • Energy: −1 ε per touching pair of oily links, plus a screened Coulomb energy 2 ε qiqj e−r/λ/r between charged links, λ from 5 sites (pure water) to 0.5 (very salty).
