Self-assembly

How do lipid membranes assemble themselves? And what does heat, or acid, do to them?

Each lipid here is a head (blue) and a two-bead tail (light). Tails stick to tails; heads only repel. Scatter a thousand of them at random and let them go: nobody tells them to build anything.

Why they assemble

A tail surrounded by other tails has lower energy than a tail out on its own, so lipids gain by clustering their tails together. But clustering costs freedom: a lipid stuck in an aggregate can wander much less than a free one, which costs entropy. At a given temperature the balance is set by the free energy F = E − TS: when tails are sticky enough, or the temperature low enough, the energy gain wins and aggregates form. Heads cannot hide inside, so they end up on the outside, facing the surrounding water.

The shape of what forms depends on the shape of the lipid. Lipids whose heads are about as wide as their tails pack into flat sheets, two lipids thick with tails inside: bilayers (in this 2D world, a bilayer is a strip). Make the heads bigger (as when heads carry more charge and repel each other more) and the lipids become wedge-shaped; they then prefer to curl into round micelles. Real cell membranes are bilayers of lipids with two long tails.

Energy or entropy?

Both, and in real water mostly entropy. This model has no water: the stickiness of tails stands in for the hydrophobic effect. In real water, oily tails are not strongly attracted to each other; rather, water molecules next to an oily tail lose freedom (they must arrange their hydrogen bonds around it), and gathering the tails together frees that water. So the force that builds membranes comes largely from the entropy of the surrounding water. A model that includes the water explicitly could show that directly; this one folds it into the tail attraction.

Too hot, too acidic

Raise the temperature and entropy wins: aggregates shed lipids and eventually fall apart. Real membranes do not dissolve at body temperature, but they do melt from an ordered gel to a fluid state, and organisms adjust their lipids to stay fluid. Acidity changes the charge on lipid heads (protonating them), which changes how strongly they repel each other. Here that is the head-size slider: smaller, less repulsive heads pack into flatter sheets; larger ones favour micelles. Real membranes made of lipids with charged heads change their structure with pH in this way.

Caveats

  • A 2D, three-bead coarse-grained lipid (in the spirit of the Cooke–Deserno model), with implicit solvent: a heat bath acts on every bead, and the solvent's hydrophobic effect is an effective attraction between tails.
  • Head size stands in for electrostatic repulsion between charged heads; there are no explicit charges or ions.
  • Assembly is slow at low temperature: aggregates can get stuck in imperfect shapes.
