The evidence for atoms: C. T. R. Wilson's cloud chamber, 1911

Can you see the path of a single atom?

Not the atom, but its wake. An α particle (a helium nucleus) shot out of radium tears electrons off the air molecules it passes; in air supersaturated with water, each of those ions grows a droplet, and the path shows as a white line. Wilson photographed them: straight, thickening towards the end, and now and then bent sharply, where the α particle has hit an atom's nucleus head on.

Run, then fire. The α particle flies almost straight through the air, losing a little energy to every molecule it ionises, and more the slower it goes, so its track thickens before it stops. Fire a few: some will turn sharply, and the molecule they hit recoils.

Why the tracks are straight

An α particle is about 7,000 times heavier than an electron, and almost all of its energy goes on knocking electrons off the molecules it passes: each one costs it about 35 eV, out of the 5 million it starts with, so it barely swerves. It loses more per centimetre the slower it goes (it spends longer near each molecule: Bethe's formula, dE/dx ∝ 1/v²), so it makes most of its ions just before it stops, a few centimetres from the source: every α particle from the same radioactive atom has the same range.

Why some tracks bend

The molecules' mass is in their nuclei, which take up a hundred-thousandth of their width. An α particle passes through millions of atoms between nuclear hits, but when it does come close to a nucleus it can be turned through a large angle, and the nucleus recoils, making a short track of its own: the forks in Wilson's photographs. Counting how often α particles turned through large angles in gold foil is how Rutherford found the nucleus, in 1911.

Perrin (Atoms, §119) took the tracks as the most direct evidence of all: here was one atom, its path drawn out. Counting the ions along a track, and the tracks themselves, gives N again (see counting atoms one at a time).

Caveats

  • The α particle's collisions with nuclei are real molecular dynamics (a tiny hard core, 0.04 σ across, inside each air molecule); its slowing by ionisation is a rule added by the page: at each look it loses C/v² of energy per σ travelled, making one ion per 0.2 ε lost, and the air molecules along that stretch are marked as ions (as many as are there). The energy it loses leaves the simulation (it would go into the electrons).
  • No water vapour: each ion is drawn as a droplet where it was made, which stays put (as in a photograph) while the ionised molecule itself moves on.
  • Scaled down: an α particle here is 7 times lighter than an air molecule (really 7, so that is right) but only about 300 times as energetic as the air's molecules, and its range is about 150 molecules.
