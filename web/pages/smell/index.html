Diffusion is slow over long distances

Each smell molecule is knocked about by collisions and wanders at random. Wandering at random does not get you far: after a time t the typical distance from where you started grows only as √t, not as t. In one direction the spread is

spread = √(2 D t)

with D the diffusion coefficient, which the page measures from how fast the spread grows. To go twice as far takes four times as long. For a smell molecule in air D is about 0.1 cm²/s; crossing a 5 m room by diffusion alone would take about (500 cm)² / (2 × 0.1 cm²/s) ≈ 1.25 million seconds: two weeks.

So what carries smells?

Air currents. Even a gentle draught of a few centimetres per second moves a smell across a room in a minute or two: drift grows in proportion to t. Turn the draught on here and the puff's centre moves steadily while its spread barely matters. The draught also smears the puff out, because air near the walls moves more slowly than air in the middle (Taylor dispersion): in still air the puff stays round, in moving air it stretches into a streak. Convection from warm bodies, heaters and cooking keeps indoor air moving all the time, which is why you smell the toast in the next room within seconds. (Here the draught takes a while to get going: the push accelerates the gas, and friction with the walls only gradually sets up a steady flow.)

Caveats

  • 2D, and the smell molecules are the same as the gas molecules apart from their colour (real odour molecules are bigger and diffuse a little more slowly).
  • The draught is driven by a steady push on every molecule; the corridor's walls are kept at a fixed temperature to carry away the heat the flow makes.
